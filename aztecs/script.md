### 0. Cold open

[curious] November 1519. A few hundred Spanish soldiers walk onto a road built across a lake. Ahead of them, on an island, stands a city. Temples, straight canals, canoes everywhere. About two hundred thousand people live there, four times as many as in London.

The people who built it rule five or six million subjects. Two hundred years earlier they were wanderers nobody wanted as neighbours. Two years from now their city will be rubble.

In the next twenty minutes: farms built on water, money that grows on trees, a school for every child, a calendar that threatens to end the world every fifty-two years, and a conquest that was not won by five hundred Spaniards.

[serious] So who were the Aztecs, and how did they rise and fall so fast? Let's find out. It takes twenty minutes.

### 1. The People with the Wrong Name

[warm, confident narrator] Start with the name. The people in this story never called themselves Aztecs. They called themselves the Mexica. And yes, that is where the word Mexico comes from.

Aztec comes from Aztlan, the homeland of their legends, somewhere far to the north. Nobody knows where it was, or whether it was a real place at all. The name Aztec only caught on in the nineteenth century, when the German scientist Alexander von Humboldt and the American historian William Prescott made it popular. We will use both names.

According to their own story, the Mexica leave Aztlan on the orders of their god Huitzilopochtli, god of the sun and of war. Priests carry his image, and he speaks through them. The march lasts about two hundred years.

They speak Nahuatl. So do many of the peoples already living in the place they are heading for: the Valley of Mexico. It is a high basin ringed by mountains, more than two thousand two hundred metres above the sea. On its floor lie five connected lakes. No river leaves the valley, so the largest of them, Lake Texcoco, is salty.

[curious] Around 1250 the Mexica arrive. They are late. Every good stretch of shore already belongs to a city: Culhuacan in the south, Azcapotzalco in the west, Texcoco in the east.

The newcomers are poor and tough, and by most accounts the neighbours dislike them. So they take the land nobody else wants, and they fight other people's wars for a living.

### 2. The Eagle and the Cactus

[warm, confident narrator] For decades the Mexica move around the lake, serving one city and then another. But their god has promised them a sign. Somewhere they will find an eagle perched on a prickly pear cactus. There they must build.

In the traditional year 1325 they find it, on a marshy little island near the western shore of Lake Texcoco. They call the place Tenochtitlan: the place of the prickly pear cactus.

[curious] Look at the Mexican flag. The eagle is still there, on its cactus, with a snake in its beak. But the snake is a late guest. In the Codex Mendoza, painted by Aztec artists around 1541, the eagle holds nothing at all. The snake first appears in a drawing made by a Spanish friar, Diego Durán, in 1581.

The island itself looks like a bad joke. It is mud and reeds. The water around it is too salty to drink, and there is hardly room for a field.

But it has three advantages. Nobody else wants it. It is hard to attack. And it sits in the middle of a lake where everything travels by canoe.

A second group of Mexica settles the northern end of the same island and calls its town Tlatelolco. Remember that name. It will have the greatest market in the land.

Around 1375 Tenochtitlan gets its first king, Acamapichtli. His title is tlatoani, the speaker. For the next fifty years the Mexica pay tribute to the strongest city on the lake, Azcapotzalco, and fight in its wars.

### 3. A City Built on Water

[warm, confident narrator] First the Mexica need land. So they make it.

They fence off a rectangle of shallow lake with stakes and woven branches. They fill it with mud from the lake bottom and layers of water plants, and they plant trees along the edges, so the roots hold everything together. The result is called a chinampa.

People call them floating gardens, but they do not float. They are fixed to the lake bed, with canals between them for canoes. The soil is rich and never dries out.

Next comes drinking water. The lake is salty, so the Mexica build an aqueduct across it, from the springs of Chapultepec on the mainland. It has two channels, side by side.

Three long causeways tie the island to the shore: one to the north, one to the west, one to the south. Each is cut by bridges. Canoes pass underneath, and the gaps make the city very hard to storm.

[serious] One danger is left: the lake itself. In 1449 it rises and floods the city. The king asks for help from his ally Nezahualcoyotl, ruler of Texcoco.

Nezahualcoyotl designs a dike of stone and earth, up to sixteen kilometres long, right across the lake. It holds back the floods. It also keeps the salty water of the east away from the fresher water around the city.

[curious] It works for generations. There is no record of the lake flooding Tenochtitlan again until the Spanish arrive and damage the dike.

### 4. Three Cities Against One

[warm, confident narrator] Around 1427 old Tezozomoc, lord of Azcapotzalco, dies. His son Maxtla seizes the throne and tightens his grip on every city around the lake. The young Mexica king, Chimalpopoca, soon dies, possibly murdered on Maxtla's orders. The prince of Texcoco, Nezahualcoyotl, is driven into exile.

[serious] Maxtla has made too many enemies at once. The Mexica choose a new king, Itzcoatl, a soldier. He joins forces with the exiled Nezahualcoyotl and with a third city, Tlacopan. In 1428 the allies take Azcapotzalco and destroy it.

The three winners stay together. Historians call them the Triple Alliance: Tenochtitlan, Texcoco and Tlacopan. This is what we mean by the Aztec Empire.

It is never one country. It is three cities sharing out the tribute of everyone they defeat. Two fifths go to Tenochtitlan, two fifths to Texcoco, and one fifth to Tlacopan.

Beside the king stands his nephew Tlacaelel, a general who becomes the power behind the throne. He reshapes both the state and its religion. At the centre he puts war, and the god of the Mexica, Huitzilopochtli.

[curious] And the winners edit the past. A later Aztec account says that under Itzcoatl the old painted histories were burned. A new story took their place. In it, the Mexica were never unwanted wanderers. They were a chosen people, and their god had promised them an empire.

It was a bold thing to say for a city barely a hundred years old.

Then they made it true.

### 5. An Empire of Tribute

[warm, confident narrator] In 1440 Itzcoatl dies, and the first Moctezuma becomes king. This is not the famous Moctezuma. This is his great-grandfather.

Under him the armies leave the valley. They march east to the Gulf of Mexico and south into Oaxaca. The kings after him keep going. By 1502, under King Ahuitzotl, Aztec power reaches from the Gulf to the Pacific, and down the coast to Soconusco, at the edge of modern Guatemala. Four to five hundred small states. Five or six million people.

[curious] But it is a strange kind of empire. A defeated king usually keeps his throne. Life in his city goes on as before. The rule is simple: pay, twice a year, and send soldiers when asked.

We know what they paid, because the list survives. The Codex Mendoza shows the tribute of thirty-eight provinces, drawn item by item. One rich province in the hot lowlands, Tochtepec, owes sixteen hundred decorated cloaks every six months. Others send cacao beans, jaguar skins, bright feathers, beads of jade and turquoise, warrior costumes, even baskets of dried chili.

[serious] Pay, and you are left alone. Refuse, and the army comes back.

It makes the empire cheap to run and quick to grow. It also means that almost nobody inside it is loyal. Every Aztec king has to put down rebellions.

And some neighbours never give in. To the west, in the late 1470s, the Tarascans crush an Aztec army. It is the empire's one great defeat. To the east, the small state of Tlaxcala holds out, with Aztec land on every side.

Remember Tlaxcala too.

### 6. The City on the Lake

[warm, confident narrator] Now walk into Tenochtitlan as it was in 1519.

You come in along a causeway, over bridges wide enough, Cortés wrote, for ten horses side by side.

Most estimates give the city about two hundred thousand people. Some scholars say fewer, some say many more. Either way, it is the largest city ancient Mexico ever saw, and one of the largest on Earth.

In the centre stands the Great Temple, a pyramid with two shrines on top. One belongs to Huitzilopochtli, god of war and the sun. The other belongs to Tlaloc, god of rain. The temple has been rebuilt six times, each new pyramid wrapped around the old one.

Close by is the king's palace, with its gardens, an aviary and a zoo.

[curious] Then go north, to the market of Tlatelolco. Cortés claims that sixty thousand people come here every day to buy and sell. Other witnesses say twenty to forty thousand. You can buy jewels of gold and silver, cloth, and food from every province.

And you pay in chocolate. Small change is cacao beans. A price list written down in 1545, a generation after the conquest, gives the idea. One bean buys a large tomato. Three buy a turkey egg. A hundred buy a good turkey hen. For bigger purchases there are lengths of white cotton cloth.

[playful] Money that grows on trees has one problem. People fake it. An Aztec account complains about the bad cacao seller, who slips worthless wild beans in among the good ones.

### 7. Growing Up Aztec

[warm, confident narrator] Who lives in this city? At the top are the nobles. Below them is the great majority, the commoners: farmers, craftsmen and traders.

Whichever you are, you go to school.

Sons of nobles go to the calmecac, where priests teach them history, the calendar, religion and the art of war. Sons of commoners go to the telpochcalli. There is one in every neighbourhood, and it is heavy on hard work and weapons training. A gifted commoner can be picked for the nobles' school. Girls are taught too, for the roles expected of them.

Hardly any society of that time tried to school every child.

[curious] School leads to war, because war is the ladder. There is no standing army. Men are called up when a campaign begins.

A boy wears a lock of hair at the back of his neck until he takes his first captive. Then he may cut it off. More captives bring better cloaks, jewellery, feathers and rank. The best join the elite: the eagle warriors and the jaguar warriors.

The favourite weapon is a wooden club, edged with razor-sharp blades of obsidian, a volcanic glass. But the real prize in battle is an enemy taken alive.

[playful] And at home? On the table are tomatoes, chili peppers and avocados, and for those who can afford it, a bitter drink of cacao and water.

Listen to the Nahuatl words. Tomatl. Chilli. Ahuacatl. Chocolatl. Out in the hills there is a wild dog called a coyotl.

You have been speaking a little Aztec all your life.

### 8. Feeding the Sun

[serious] Why take enemies alive? This is the part of the story everybody has heard.

The Aztecs believed that the gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world. Humans owed them a debt, and the debt was paid in lives. Stop paying, and the universe would end. So people were sacrificed, most of them prisoners of war.

Two things are worth knowing. First, the Aztecs did not invent this. Human sacrifice was practised across ancient Mexico, by the Maya and by the Aztecs' enemies too.

Second, the numbers are disputed. Sources written after the conquest say that when the Great Temple was rededicated in 1487, eighty thousand four hundred prisoners died in four days. Most historians think that figure is inflated, and put the real number in the thousands.

[curious] For a long time, some scholars suspected that the Spanish had invented the worst of it. Then, in 2015, archaeologists digging beside the Great Temple found a tower built of human skulls. More than six hundred so far: men, women and children. A conquistador had described exactly such a tower.

Time itself was dangerous. The Aztecs ran two calendars at once: a solar year of three hundred and sixty-five days, and a sacred count of two hundred and sixty. The two came back to the same starting point only once every fifty-two years.

On that night, every fire in the land was put out. Priests tried to light a new flame. If it caught, the world had another fifty-two years.

[serious] A New Fire was lit in 1507. Nobody knew it was the last.

### 9. Wars of Flowers

[warm, confident narrator] Go back to Tlaxcala, the small state the empire never swallowed. The Aztecs fought it again and again, in a kind of battle with a beautiful name: the flower war.

A flower war was arranged in advance, at an agreed place and time. The warriors arrived in paint and feathers, wearing the emblems of their rank. The point was not to take land. The point was to take prisoners.

These wars began under the first Moctezuma, and they went on for generations.

[serious] For the Aztecs it was training, religion and a show of strength. For Tlaxcala there was nothing ceremonial about it. Year after year, its young men were carried off to die in Tenochtitlan.

Now you can see the whole weakness of this empire. Inside its borders are hundreds of cities that pay only because they must. Next door are enemies with every reason to want revenge. All that holds it together is fear of one city on a lake.

[warm, confident narrator] In 1502 a new king takes the throne: the second Moctezuma, the famous one. He will rule for eighteen years. He tightens the king's control and makes the empire stronger than it has ever been. As the year 1519 begins, no power in this part of the world can challenge Tenochtitlan.

[curious] Then news comes from the Gulf coast. Strange ships have arrived, with strange men on board.

Moctezuma sends messengers to look at them.

### 10. Strangers from the Sea

[warm, confident narrator] Their leader is Hernán Cortés. In February 1519 he leaves Cuba with eleven ships, about five hundred soldiers and sixteen horses. The governor of Cuba has tried to replace him, and Cortés has sailed anyway.

His first problem is language.

First he picks up Gerónimo de Aguilar, a Spaniard who was shipwrecked years before and lived as a captive among the Maya. Then, in March, after a battle on the coast, the local Maya hand over twenty young women. One of them speaks both Maya and Nahuatl. The Spanish baptize her Marina. The Nahuas call her Malintzin.

Cortés speaks Spanish to Aguilar. Aguilar speaks Maya to Malintzin. Malintzin speaks Nahuatl to everyone else. Without her, there are no alliances.

[serious] Cortés destroys his own ships, so that nobody can turn back, and in August he marches inland. Tlaxcala fights him first, and fights hard. Then it makes a decision. These strangers could be a weapon against Tenochtitlan.

On the eighth of November 1519, Cortés, his men and their Tlaxcalan allies walk along the causeway. Moctezuma comes out to meet them.

[curious] You may have heard that Moctezuma took Cortés for a returning god, Quetzalcoatl. Cortés never says so in his own letters. The story first appears in a book of 1552, by a writer who never set foot in America. Most historians now think it was invented after the conquest.

Moctezuma was doing what kings do with dangerous guests. He kept them close, and watched.

Within days he is their hostage, in his own city.

### 11. The Fall

[serious] For months there is an uneasy peace. Then, in the spring of 1520, Cortés rushes back to the coast. A second Spanish force, more than eight hundred soldiers, has come from Cuba to arrest him.

He leaves about eighty men in Tenochtitlan under Pedro de Alvarado. In May, during a religious festival at the Great Temple, Alvarado attacks the unarmed Aztec nobles and kills them. The city rises.

Cortés returns. In June, Moctezuma dies. The Spanish say he was hit by stones thrown by his own people. Aztec accounts say the Spanish killed him.

On the night of the thirtieth of June 1520 the Spaniards try to slip out along a causeway. They are caught. Cortés loses more than half his men. The Spanish call it the Night of Sorrows.

The survivors fight their way to Tlaxcala. And Tlaxcala takes them in.

Then comes smallpox. The people of Mexico have never met it. The new king, Cuitlahuac, who drove the Spaniards out, dies after eighty days on the throne. His successor, Cuauhtemoc, is about twenty-five.

In May 1521 Cortés is back. He has thirteen ships built for the lake and about a thousand Spaniards. With them march native allies in the tens of thousands. Some counts say two hundred thousand.

They cut the aqueduct. They block the causeways. The siege lasts about three months. On the thirteenth of August 1521, Cuauhtemoc is caught trying to cross the lake in a canoe. The city is in ruins.

[serious] Five hundred Spaniards did not conquer the Aztec Empire. Its own subjects and its old enemies did, with Spanish steel, Spanish horses and a disease on their side.

### 12. What Remains

[warm, confident narrator] Cortés builds a new capital on top of the ruins, using the stones of the temples. It is Mexico City. Cuauhtemoc stays a prisoner. In 1525, on a march to Honduras, Cortés has him hanged.

The lake goes too. The new rulers decide to drain it, and the work is finally finished in the late nineteenth century.

[serious] The people suffer most. The great sickness of 1545 is estimated to have killed up to eighty per cent of the population where it struck. The population of Mexico did not return to its old level until the twentieth century.

[warm, confident narrator] But the Aztecs did not vanish. In the sixteenth century, Nahua scholars working with a Spanish friar described their whole world in their own language, in the book we call the Florentine Codex. Today more than one and a half million people speak Nahuatl. And at Xochimilco, on the southern edge of Mexico City, the chinampas are still there.

[curious] On the twenty-first of February 1978, electric company workers digging in the centre of Mexico City hit a carved stone disc more than three metres across. It showed the moon goddess Coyolxauhqui, and it had been lying at the foot of the Great Temple all along. Thirteen buildings were pulled down to dig the temple out.

And every Mexican flag still carries the founding of Tenochtitlan: an eagle, on a cactus, on an island in a lake that is no longer there.
