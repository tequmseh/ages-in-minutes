### 0. Cold open

Here is what most people know about Carthage. It had elephants. It had Hannibal. It lost. And when it lost, the Romans ploughed salt into the ground so that nothing would ever grow there again.

The salt never happened. No ancient writer mentions it. The story appears in the nineteenth century.

But notice something. Even the legend about Carthage was written by somebody else.

Carthage was a great city for more than 600 years. It had libraries. Almost nothing from them survives. Nearly every sentence we have about it comes from Greeks, who fought it, and Romans, who burned it. Imagine if the only books about your country were written by the two nations that hated it most.

So what can you really know about a superpower when its enemies wrote its biography?

Let's find out. It takes 20 minutes.

### 1. The Purple People

Start on this strip of coast, in what is now Lebanon. Mountains behind, sea in front, and almost no room to farm. The people here live in a handful of port cities: Tyre, Sidon, Byblos. They call themselves Canaanites. The Greeks call them Phoenicians, a name that probably comes from the Greek word for purple.

Purple is their luxury brand. The dye is made from sea snails, thousands of them for a single robe, and making it stinks. It is also worth more than gold, so nobody complains. Centuries later Roman emperors will keep the colour for themselves.

They sell cedar wood from the mountains, glass, and fine metalwork. And they sell one thing by accident. Phoenician traders keep their accounts with 22 simple signs, one for each sound. The Greeks borrow the signs and add vowels. The Romans borrow them from the Greeks. You are reading the grandchild of that system right now. The Greek word for book comes from the name of Byblos. So, later, does the word Bible.

And they sail. West, mostly, because the west has metal: silver and tin in Spain, at the far end of the sea. That is a long trip in a small ship. You need places to stop, repair and trade along the way. Their ships are the best afloat. When the kings of Persia need a navy, they do not build one. They hire the Phoenicians. But at home the Phoenicians do not build an empire. They build a route.

One of the stops on that route is about to outgrow its parents.

### 2. New City

The legend goes like this. A princess of Tyre, Elissa, has a brother who is king and a husband who is rich. The brother murders the husband for his money. Elissa loads the treasure onto ships and runs.

She lands in North Africa and asks the local king for a little land: only as much as an oxhide can cover. He agrees. She cuts the hide into the thinnest possible strips, ties them end to end, and circles an entire hill. Mathematicians still call the question of how to enclose the most land with a fixed length of rope Dido's problem.

It is a great story. It was written down by a Roman-era author about nine hundred years later. And the Roman poet Virgil turns Elissa into Dido and gives her a doomed love affair with the ancestor of Rome. So even the founding myth of Carthage reaches us as Roman literature.

What we can check is this. Greek historians put the founding in 814 BC. The oldest finds on the site are from the 700s. Close enough.

And the name is real. Qart-hadasht. It means New City. Newtown. New, that is, compared with Tyre. For centuries Carthage keeps sending gifts to the great temple back in Tyre, like a grown-up child posting money home.

Now look where Newtown is. Here, in modern Tunisia, where Africa almost touches Sicily. This is the narrow waist of the Mediterranean. Every ship going east or west has to pass through this gap. Carthage is a tollbooth on the only road.

### 3. The Trading Empire

Back home, things go badly. Tyre is squeezed by one eastern empire after another: Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians. The mother city fades, and the daughter inherits the family business.

By about 500 BC Carthage runs a network. Western Sicily. Sardinia. Ibiza. The coast of Spain, where the silver is. The coast of Morocco. Notice what kind of empire this is. It is not provinces and governors. It is harbours, warehouses and contracts. Carthage does not want your land. It wants to be the only one allowed to sell you things. Foreign ships caught in its waters are sunk.

They go a long way to find customers. A captain named Hanno sails out through the Strait of Gibraltar and down the coast of West Africa. His report mentions rivers full of crocodiles, a mountain of fire, and an island of hairy, violent people whom the interpreters call Gorillai. His men kill three and bring the skins home. That report is where the word gorilla comes from.

Another captain, Himilco, goes north along the Atlantic coast toward the tin of Britain. He comes back with tales of sea monsters and weeds that trap ships. Possibly true. Possibly meant to scare off the competition.

And in 509 BC Carthage signs a treaty with a small Italian town that has just thrown out its king. The treaty tells the Romans where their ships may not go. The Romans sign.

Remember who is the senior partner here. It will not last.

### 4. The Sicilian Wars

Sicily is the problem. The west of the island is Carthaginian. The east is Greek, led by the city of Syracuse. Neither side can push the other into the sea.

In 480 BC a Carthaginian army is destroyed at Himera. The Greeks like to say it happened on the same day they beat the Persians at Salamis. Seventy years later Carthage comes back and wipes Himera off the map. That is the pattern for almost two centuries: war, truce, and war again. Cities are sacked. Nobody wins.

But the Greeks do one thing the Carthaginians never manage. They write it down, and their books survive. In those books Carthaginians are cruel, greedy and cannot be trusted. The Romans will later make it a proverb. Punic faith: their phrase for treachery. Which is a little rich coming from Rome, as we are about to see.

Then, in 310 BC, a ruler of Syracuse named Agathocles tries something new. He is under siege at home. So he slips out with about 14,000 men, sails to Africa, burns his own ships so that nobody can leave, and marches on Carthage itself.

He loses in the end. But he has found the weak spot. Carthage is a giant at sea and soft at home. Its farmland is undefended, and its African subjects do not love it.

The Romans will read about this.

### 5. How It Worked

So what was this city like from the inside? Our best witness is, again, a Greek. Aristotle studies the constitution of Carthage and calls it one of the best in the world, a balance of kingship, aristocracy and democracy.

Two chief magistrates, elected every year from the richest families. A senate. And a court of 104 judges whose job is to watch the generals. Lose a battle through your own fault, and the court can have you crucified. It keeps the generals honest. It also keeps them nervous.

The army is hired. Libyan infantry, Numidian horsemen, Spaniards, Gauls, slingers from the Balearic Islands. Citizens row the fleet and run the business. For a city of merchants this makes sense: citizens are too valuable to waste in battle. The system has one flaw. Hired soldiers expect to be paid. In its final form the city has a round naval harbour with covered docks for 220 warships.

The farms are famous. A Carthaginian named Mago writes a manual on agriculture in 28 books. Keep him in mind.

And then there is the hardest question. Greek and Roman authors say the Carthaginians burned their own children as offerings to the gods. Archaeologists have found a sacred precinct with thousands of urns holding the cremated bones of infants and lambs. One team of scientists reads the bones as babies who died before or soon after birth. Another says the ages point to sacrifice. The argument is not over.

The accusers were enemies. The urns are real. Hold both.

### 6. The First War with Rome

In 264 BC a gang of mercenaries holding the Sicilian town of Messana asks for protection. They ask Carthage. They also ask Rome. Both say yes, and two powers that have been partners for 250 years are suddenly facing each other across a narrow strait.

Rome has a problem. It has the best infantry in Italy and no navy. Then a Carthaginian warship runs aground. The Romans take it apart, copy it, and build 120 ships. According to Polybius, the crews learn to row sitting on benches on dry land while the hulls are still being finished.

They still cannot sail them well. So they cheat. Each ship gets a hinged bridge with a spike on the end. Drop it on the enemy deck, and a sea battle becomes a land battle.

In 256 BC, off Cape Ecnomus, about 680 ships and perhaps 290,000 men fight. It may be the largest naval battle in history. Rome wins.

Then Rome invades Africa, as Agathocles did. Its general offers terms so harsh that Carthage decides to keep fighting, hires a Spartan professional, and wipes the invasion out. Then the weather joins in. One storm sinks 384 Roman ships and drowns about 100,000 men.

Rome builds another fleet. And another. In 241 BC Carthage gives up Sicily and agrees to pay 3,200 talents of silver.

Twenty-three years. Carthage fights like a company: when the costs get too high, it settles. Rome does not settle. That difference will decide everything that follows.

### 7. The Truceless War

Now the bill arrives. Carthage has lost Sicily, owes Rome a fortune, and has an army of mercenaries coming home to be paid.

It tries to bargain them down. Bad idea. The soldiers mutiny and march on the city. The African towns, tired of Carthaginian taxes, join them. Within months the power that ruled the western sea is under siege by its own employees. To survive, it has to do something it has avoided for generations: hand weapons to its own citizens.

The historian Polybius calls this the Truceless War, because neither side takes prisoners. There are mutilations and crucifixions on both sides. It lasts more than three years, and for a while it looks as if Carthage will simply cease to exist.

The man who finally wins it is a general from the Sicilian war: Hamilcar Barca. In the last battle he traps the rebel army in a mountain pass and starves it.

And while Carthage is fighting for its life, Rome makes a move. It seizes Sardinia and Corsica. When Carthage protests, Rome threatens war and adds another 1,200 talents to the bill.

Carthage is in no state to refuse. It pays.

Many historians think this is the moment that makes the next war certain. The first war Carthage lost fairly. This is robbery, and everyone in Carthage knows it.

Hamilcar knows it best.

### 8. The Barcas in Spain

In 237 BC Hamilcar leaves for Spain. Before he goes, he takes his nine-year-old son to the altar and makes him swear never to be a friend of Rome. We know this because the son told the story himself, decades later, to a foreign king.

The son's name is Hannibal.

Spain has what Carthage needs: silver mines and tough soldiers. In a few years the Barca family builds what is almost a private kingdom, far from the politicians at home. The silver pays off Rome. It also pays for a new army, trained in a long series of Spanish wars.

Hamilcar dies in battle. His son-in-law takes over and founds a capital on the Spanish coast. He calls it Qart-hadasht. Yes, another Newtown. The Romans call it New Carthage. Today it is Cartagena.

Rome gets nervous and draws a line on the map: Carthaginians stay south of the River Ebro.

In 221 BC the son-in-law is murdered, and the army chooses a new commander. Hannibal is 26. He has grown up in a military camp. He has never really lived in Carthage.

South of the Ebro there is a town called Saguntum. It is on the Carthaginian side of the line. It is also a friend of Rome. Hannibal besieges it for eight months and takes it.

A Roman envoy arrives in Carthage. He gathers a fold of his toga and says: in here I carry peace and war. Choose. The senators shout back: you choose. He lets the fold drop. War.

### 9. Hannibal

Rome plans to fight this war in Spain and in Africa. Hannibal has other plans. He will bring the war to Italy, and since Rome now rules the sea, he will walk there.

Across the Pyrenees. Across the Rhône. And in the autumn of 218 BC, across the Alps, with an army and about three dozen elephants.

He comes down the other side with 20,000 infantry and 6,000 cavalry. He has lost about half his men. As for the elephants, nearly all are dead by the end of that winter. The most famous animals in military history barely fight.

It does not matter. Hannibal beats one Roman army at the River Trebia. In the morning mist at Lake Trasimene he ambushes another and destroys it on the shore. About 15,000 Romans die there, the consul among them.

Then, in 216 BC, at Cannae, Rome sends about 86,000 men against his 50,000. Hannibal lets his centre bend backwards. The Romans push in, sure they are winning. His wings close around them. The Roman historian Livy counts about 48,000 Roman dead in one afternoon. So many are aristocrats that Hannibal's brother later pours a heap of their gold rings onto the floor of the senate in Carthage.

That evening his cavalry commander begs him to march on Rome at once. Hannibal says no. According to Livy, the officer answers: you know how to win a victory, Hannibal. You do not know how to use one.

Historians have argued about that decision ever since.

### 10. The Long Defeat

By every rule of ancient war, Rome should now ask for terms. Rome refuses even to ransom its prisoners.

Instead it changes tactics. A general named Fabius simply stops fighting battles. He follows Hannibal around, cuts off his food, and waits. The Romans nickname him the Delayer. It is not a compliment yet.

Hannibal stays in Italy for fifteen years. He is never beaten there. Some of Rome's allies come over to him, including Capua, the second city of Italy. Most do not. And the government in Carthage sends him very little.

Meanwhile Rome goes after everything else. A young general named Scipio takes Spain and its silver. Hannibal's brother brings a second army over the Alps and is killed before the two can meet. The Romans throw his head into Hannibal's camp.

Then Scipio does what Agathocles did. He lands in Africa.

Carthage calls Hannibal home. In 202 BC the two generals meet at Zama. Before the battle they talk face to face. It changes nothing. This time the Numidian cavalry, the best horsemen in Africa, rides on the Roman side. Hannibal loses the first big battle of his life, and it is the only one that counts.

The terms: no overseas empire. Almost no fleet. No war without Rome's permission. And 10,000 talents, paid over fifty years.

Carthage is now a city with a farm attached.

### 11. Carthage Must Be Destroyed

Here is the part the Romans did not expect. Carthage recovers.

Hannibal goes into politics, audits the treasury, and shows that the city can pay Rome without raising taxes. His rivals report him to the Romans. He flees and dies in exile, by poison, around 183 BC.

But the city keeps getting richer. In 151 BC it pays off the last instalment.

A Roman senator named Cato has seen it and cannot stop talking about it. One day he lets a fresh African fig fall from his toga. This, he says, grew three days' sail from Rome. And according to Plutarch he ends every vote, on any subject, the same way: Carthage must be destroyed. A rival senator ends his the opposite way: Carthage must be spared. Cato wins.

Rome's ally in Numidia keeps seizing Carthaginian land. Carthage finally fights back. That breaks the treaty.

In 149 BC a Roman army lands. Carthage surrenders 200,000 sets of armour and 2,000 catapults. Only then does it hear the real demand: abandon the city.

It refuses and rearms from nothing. Statues are melted down for blades. The women are said to cut off their hair to make ropes for new catapults. The city holds out for three years. In 146 BC the Romans break in. Six days of street fighting. Fifty thousand survivors sold. The city burns.

The Roman commander watches, weeps, and quotes Homer on the fall of Troy. The historian Polybius is standing next to him.

### 12. What Remains

A century later the Romans rebuild Carthage. They cannot help it. The site is too good. Roman Carthage becomes the second city of the western empire.

The language outlives the state by five hundred years. Around AD 400, Saint Augustine reports that farmers near his town still speak Punic.

The books do not survive. After the fire, the libraries of Carthage are handed to African kings and vanish. Rome keeps exactly one work: Mago's manual on farming. The Senate orders it translated into Latin. Roman authors quote it for centuries, and pieces of it can still be read.

So what do we know about Carthage?

We know what its enemies wanted us to know: cruel, greedy, treacherous. And we know what they could not hide. A constitution that a Greek philosopher admired. An alphabet the whole West still uses. A navy that Rome had to copy plank by plank. A general whose name Roman parents used to frighten their children, and whose statue the Romans later put up in their own city. A city so well placed that its destroyers built it again.

And one book.

The people later accused of sowing that land with salt are the ones who saved the manual on how to make it grow.
