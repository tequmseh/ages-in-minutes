### 0. Cold open

[curious] Cleopatra lived closer in time to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid. To her, the pyramids were already ancient history: further back than she is from us.

That is how long Egypt lasted. One state, with the same gods, the same script and the same idea of a king, for three thousand years. Almost nothing else in history comes close.

In the next twenty minutes: a river that builds a country, three collapses and three comebacks, a woman who makes herself king, a pharaoh who tries to abolish the old gods, and the people who really built the pyramids. They were not slaves. They ate beef, and they named their work gangs. [playful] One was called the Drunkards of Menkaure.

So how do you make a country last three thousand years? Let's find out. It takes twenty minutes.

### 1. The Gift

[warm, confident narrator] Start with the map. This is the Sahara, the biggest hot desert on earth. And this thin green line is the Nile. Egypt is that line: a country a thousand kilometres long and rarely more than twenty kilometres wide.

Every summer, rain falls on the highlands of Ethiopia, far to the south, and the river floods. When the water goes down it leaves a layer of black mud, the best farmland in the ancient world, delivered free, once a year. The Egyptians call their country Kemet, the Black Land. Everything else is Deshret, the Red Land.

But the gift is not reliable. Too low a flood, and people starve. Too high, and it sweeps villages away. The oldest royal annals we have, carved around 2400 BC, record one number for almost every year: how high the river rose.

The geography does a second job. Desert on both sides. Sea to the north. Rapids to the south. Egypt comes with its walls already built.

And the river runs north while the wind blows south. Drift downstream, raise a sail to come back. [playful] Egypt gets a motorway with free fuel in both directions.

A Greek visitor, Herodotus, called this land the gift of the river. He was right. But a gift like this needs managing. Someone has to measure the flood, store the grain, and settle who owns which field when the water goes down. That someone is about to appear.

### 2. Two Lands

[serious] Around 3100 BC there are two Egypts. Upper Egypt is the valley, in the south. Yes, upper is at the bottom of the map: up means upstream. Lower Egypt is the Delta, in the north.

Then a southern king conquers the north. We have what looks like his victory poster, a carved stone the size of a serving tray. On one side King Narmer wears the white crown of the south and is about to club a prisoner. On the other he wears the red crown of the north and inspects two rows of headless enemies.

From now on every king wears both crowns, one inside the other, and calls himself Lord of the Two Lands. Egypt never forgets that it is two countries held together by one man. It matters every time the man is weak.

The new state builds its capital at the join, Memphis, right where valley meets Delta. And it has a new tool. Writing appears in Egypt at about the same moment as the state. The earliest examples we have are small labels tied to goods in royal tombs. [playful] Hieroglyphs begin as stock control.

The king is not just a ruler. He is a god on earth, and he has one job: to keep maat. Order, balance, the flood on time, everything in its place. The opposite of maat is chaos. Hold on to that idea. It is the operating manual for the next three thousand years.

### 3. The Pyramid Age

[curious] If the king is a god, his tomb is a public works project.

Around 2650 BC King Djoser's architect, Imhotep, the first architect whose name we know, tries something new. Royal tombs until now are mud-brick boxes. Imhotep builds one in stone, then stacks five more on top. This is the Step Pyramid: the oldest big stone building we know of.

A century later King Sneferu wants smooth sides, and he learns the hard way. One of his pyramids ends as a ruin. Another starts too steep; halfway up, the builders change the angle. [playful] It is still there, and it is still bent. The third one works. Sneferu moves more stone than any other Egyptian king, and what he really builds is a workforce that knows how.

His son Khufu cashes in. The Great Pyramid: two point three million blocks, a hundred and forty-six metres high. For nearly four thousand years nothing on earth is taller.

Who built it? Not slaves. We have found their town next door: bakeries, barracks for about two thousand men, and cattle bones by the ton. The crews rotate in from villages across Egypt, a kind of labour tax, and are fed like soldiers.

And we have a diary. In 2013 archaeologists found the oldest inscribed papyri in the world. They belong to an inspector called Merer. With forty men he ferries limestone down the Nile to Giza, about two hundred blocks a month.

The Great Pyramid is a logistics miracle with a mountain on top.

### 4. The First Collapse

[serious] Then it stops.

The pyramids get smaller and shoddier. The money is going elsewhere: every dead king needs priests and estates to keep his cult running forever, tax-free. Forever turns out to be expensive.

Power drains out of the capital too. Provincial governors start passing their jobs from father to son, and building grand tombs at home instead of next to the king.

The last great king of the age, Pepi the Second, comes to the throne as a small boy and reigns, by tradition, for more than ninety years. Historians doubt the number, but not the problem: a very long reign, an old king, and heirs who die waiting.

And then, it seems, the river fails. Around 2200 BC the climate shifts across the whole region, and the Nile floods run low for years. The king has one job. He can no longer do it.

The state breaks in two, north against south, just as the two crowns always warned. Later Egyptians remember it as the world turned upside down. One text complains that the poor have become rich, and that a woman who used to look at her face in the water now owns a mirror. [playful] Note who is complaining.

For about a century there is no Egypt. There are warlords with good PR. One governor, Ankhtifi, boasts in his tomb that when the whole south was dying of hunger, he let no one in his province starve.

### 5. The Reboot

[warm, confident narrator] And here is the first clue to our question. Egypt has collapsed. It does not turn into something else. It reboots.

Around 2055 BC a southern king from a backwater town called Thebes fights his way north and reunites the Two Lands. His name is Mentuhotep, and later Egyptians rank him with Narmer: founder number two.

His successors move the capital back north, close to Memphis, because you cannot run the whole river from one end. And they have learned something. Look at the statues of Senusret the Third. The pyramid kings look like serene gods. This one looks tired. Heavy eyelids, a lined face, big ears: a king who listens and does not sleep. It is a royal portrait built to say, this is a hard job.

They cut the governors down to size. And in the south they push into Nubia, where the gold is, and plant a chain of mud-brick fortresses along the river, with walls ten metres high. [playful] One of them is called, simply, Subduer of the Nubians.

This is also when Egypt writes its classics. Stories, advice, complaints, and the tale of Sinuhe, an exile who makes a fortune abroad and still begs to come home to be buried. For Egyptians this period, not the age of the pyramids, is the golden age: the time when the language was perfect.

It lasts four hundred years. Then the pattern repeats.

### 6. Foreign Kings

[serious] The second collapse comes from an unexpected direction: immigration.

For generations, people from the Levant have been settling in the eastern Delta. Traders, sailors, soldiers. When the state weakens again, their leaders simply take over the north. Egyptians call them heqa khasut, rulers of foreign lands. The Greeks will garble that into Hyksos.

Later Egyptian propaganda paints this as a savage invasion. Archaeology at their capital, Avaris, shows something slower and quieter: a mixed town that grew until it ran the place.

And they bring new technology. The horse and chariot. A stronger bow. New bronze weapons. Egypt has been so safe for so long that it has fallen behind.

The south, ruled from Thebes, is squeezed between the Hyksos in the north and a Nubian kingdom in the south. And Thebes fights. We have the body of one Theban king, Seqenenre. His skull has several wounds, made by weapons of a Levantine type. He seems to have died with his hands tied.

His successors finish the job. King Kamose's men catch a messenger in the desert, carrying a letter from the Hyksos king to the king of Nubia: attack from the south, and we will share Egypt. The alliance never happens. Around 1550 BC Ahmose takes Avaris and chases the Hyksos into Palestine. And Egypt draws a conclusion that changes everything: the desert walls are not enough. From now on, defence means owning the neighbours.

### 7. The Empire

[confident] Welcome to the New Kingdom, and to Egypt as a superpower.

The king now has a standing army with chariots, and he uses it. Thutmose the Third fights seventeen campaigns in twenty years. At Megiddo he takes his army single file through a narrow mountain pass, against the advice of his generals, and comes out where the enemy is not expecting him. Egypt now runs from Syria to deep inside Sudan.

Before him comes someone stranger. Hatshepsut is his stepmother and his regent. Then she declares herself king. Not queen: king, with the full titles and, on her statues, the royal false beard. She rules for about twenty years, sends a trading fleet to the land of Punt for incense trees, and builds one of the most elegant temples in Egypt. Long after her death her name is chiselled off the walls. [playful] It didn't work. We're talking about her.

The money pours into one place: Thebes, city of the god Amun. His temple at Karnak grows into one of the largest religious sites ever built. His priests grow with it. Keep an eye on them.

The kings stop building pyramids, which are basically signposts for robbers. They cut hidden tombs into a desert valley instead. We call it the Valley of the Kings.

And the king gets a nickname. People refer to the palace instead of the man: per-aa, the Great House. The way reporters say the White House. It reaches us, through the Bible, as pharaoh.

### 8. The Heretic

[curious] At the height of all this wealth, one pharaoh tries to rewrite the operating manual.

Around 1350 BC Amenhotep the Fourth announces that there is really only one god worth worshipping: the Aten, the disc of the sun. He renames himself Akhenaten. He shuts down the temples of Amun and has the old god's name hacked off monuments all over Egypt. Then he moves the court to a brand-new capital in the desert.

Is this the world's first monotheism? Perhaps. [playful] It is certainly a hostile takeover of the richest institution in the country.

The art changes overnight. After centuries of perfect, frozen kings, Akhenaten has himself shown with a long face, a pot belly and wide hips, kissing his children. His wife, Nefertiti, gets the most famous portrait in Egyptian history.

Meanwhile the empire's inbox fills up. We have it: about three hundred and fifty letters on clay tablets, from foreign kings and vassals. The kings want gold.

Akhenaten dies after seventeen years. Within a few years it is all reversed. The new city is abandoned. The old gods return. The boy who signs the restoration is called Tutankhamun, and he dies at about nineteen. His successors scrub the whole family from the king lists.

Which is exactly why you know his name. His small tomb is forgotten, and the robbers who emptied the other royal tombs mostly miss it. In 1922 it is found almost intact. The most famous pharaoh of all is the one Egypt tried to delete.

### 9. The Superpower

[confident] The generals who clean up afterwards found a new dynasty, and its star is Ramesses the Second. He reigns for sixty-six years. He has around a hundred children. And he puts his name on everything, even other kings' buildings.

In 1274 BC he marches into Syria to fight the other superpower of the age, the Hittites, at Kadesh. It may be the biggest chariot battle ever fought: thousands of them. Ramesses walks into an ambush and is saved by reinforcements at the last moment. The result is a draw.

Back home he carves it on temple walls all over Egypt as a personal triumph: Ramesses alone in his chariot, mowing down Hittites.

Fifteen years later comes the interesting part. Egypt and the Hittites sign a peace treaty: no more war, a defence pact, and the return of each other's refugees. Both sides' versions survive. It is the oldest surviving treaty of its kind, and a copy hangs at the United Nations.

He also carves four statues of himself, twenty metres high, into a cliff at Abu Simbel. [playful] Subtle it is not.

The best documents of his reign come from a village: Deir el-Medina, home of the men who cut the royal tombs. They could read, and they wrote on flakes of stone. One flake is an attendance register for forty workers. Absent: ill. Absent: stung by a scorpion. Absent: embalming his brother. Absent: brewing beer. We have the sick notes of the men who built the Valley of the Kings.

### 10. The Long Decline

[warm, confident narrator] The third collapse is the slow one.

Around 1177 BC the whole eastern Mediterranean comes apart. The Hittite empire vanishes. Raiders the Egyptians call the peoples of the sea come down the coast. Ramesses the Third stops them in the Delta and carves the victory on his temple. Egypt survives. It is nearly the only great power that does.

But survival is expensive. The empire in Syria is gone. Grain prices climb. And in the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses the Third, the tomb builders of Deir el-Medina do not get their rations. So they put down their tools and sit down behind a temple. The scribe writes down what they shout: we are hungry. It is the first recorded strike in history. It works; they get paid. Then the pay is late again.

After that the signs pile up. A conspiracy in the royal harem ends with the king's throat cut. The priests of Amun quietly dig up the great pharaohs, strip what gold is left, and hide the bodies together for safety.

By 1070 BC the high priest of Amun runs the south like a king. The two crowns have split again.

Then come the outsiders. Libyan chiefs rule as pharaohs. Then kings from Nubia conquer Egypt from the south, and here is the twist: they come to restore proper religion, and they build pyramids, centuries after Egypt stopped. There are more pyramids in Sudan today than in Egypt. Then, in 663 BC, the Assyrians sack Thebes.

### 11. Other People's Egypt

[warm, confident narrator] From here on, Egypt is the prize rather than the player. And yet look at what happens to everyone who wins it.

In 525 BC the Persians arrive. Cambyses takes Egypt, and Darius has himself shown as a pharaoh, building Egyptian temples.

In 332 BC Alexander the Great walks in without a fight and founds a city on the coast named after himself. After his death one of his generals, Ptolemy, takes Egypt as his share.

The Ptolemies are Macedonian Greeks. [playful] They speak Greek, marry their sisters, and run the country from Alexandria like a family firm. Alexandria becomes the greatest city of its age: a lighthouse a hundred metres tall, and a library that tries to own a copy of every book in the world.

But up the river they build temples in pure Egyptian style, with themselves on the walls as pharaohs. Several of the best-preserved temples in Egypt are theirs.

In 196 BC the priests issue a decree thanking a teenage Ptolemy for his tax breaks. They carve it in three scripts: hieroglyphs for the gods, everyday Egyptian for the clerks, Greek for the government. Remember that stone.

The last of the line is Cleopatra the Seventh. She is clever and ruthless, and the first of her family to bother learning Egyptian. She bets on two Romans in a row. The second one loses. In 30 BC she takes her own life, and Egypt becomes the personal property of Octavian, soon to be Rome's first emperor.

### 12. What Remains

[warm] Even Roman emperors who never set foot in Egypt appear on temple walls as pharaohs.

What finally ends ancient Egypt is not an army. It is a change of mind. Egypt becomes Christian, the temples lose their funding, and the priests who can read the old script die off. In AD 394, on the island of Philae, a priest carves a short text beside the figure of a god. It is the last hieroglyphic inscription we know. Then the signs go silent for fourteen hundred years.

In 1799 French soldiers rebuilding a fort find a broken slab with three scripts on it: that decree for the teenage king. Because one version is Greek, scholars finally have a key. In 1822 a Frenchman, Champollion, cracks it, and Egypt starts talking again.

So. How do you make a country last three thousand years?

Start with a river that feeds you and deserts that guard you. Add one idea simple enough to survive anything: there is an order to the world, and the king's job is to keep it. Then write everything down, so that when it all falls apart, and it will, three times, the next people know what to rebuild.

Libyans, Nubians, Persians, Greeks and Romans all conquered Egypt. And every one of them put on the double crown and tried to be Egyptian.
