### 0. Cold open

[curious] Look at any clock. Sixty seconds in a minute, sixty minutes in an hour. You are counting the way people counted in southern Iraq four thousand years ago.

The same people built the first cities, some of the oldest writing on Earth, the first written laws and the oldest epic poem. And one of the oldest personal names in history belongs not to a king, but to an accountant.

In the next twenty minutes: a baby in a basket who becomes the first emperor, the oldest customer complaint on record, a party for sixty-nine thousand guests, a library that survived because it burned, and a tower that became the Tower of Babel.

[serious] So how did a land of mud and reeds invent so much of our world? Let's find out. It takes twenty minutes.

### 1. Between the Rivers

[warm, confident narrator] Start with the map. Two rivers come down from the mountains of what is now Turkey: the Euphrates and the Tigris. They run south-east, side by side, and empty into the Persian Gulf. The Greeks called the land between them Mesopotamia: between the rivers. Today it is mostly Iraq.

The south of it is a strange place to start a civilization. Almost no rain falls. There is no building stone, almost no timber, and no metal at all. What it has is water, mud and reeds.

And the water is dangerous. The Nile floods gently every summer, right on time. The Tigris and Euphrates flood in spring, in April and May, just as the barley is ripening. Some years they wash the fields away.

So the people here learn to fight the rivers. They dig canals, raise banks and store water for the dry months. Irrigated, this mud is some of the richest farmland on Earth.

By about 5000 BC villages stand all over the south. Their houses are made of mud brick, and so are their temples. The Sumerians later said that kingship first came down from heaven at one of them: Eridu, near the shore of the Gulf.

[curious] Mud brick crumbles and has to be rebuilt. So each generation builds a new temple on top of the old one. At Eridu, archaeologists dug through some eighteen temples, stacked one on top of another.

### 2. The First City

[warm, confident narrator] Around 3500 BC one of these towns starts to grow like nothing before it. Uruk, on the Euphrates.

By 3100 BC up to fifty thousand people live here, and many more in the villages around it. It is the largest city on Earth.

In the middle stand the temples of the sky god An and the goddess Inanna, raised on platforms high above the rooftops. The temple collects the harvest and hands out rations of barley, oil and beer.

[curious] And that creates a problem. How do you keep track of thousands of jars and hundreds of workers? Memory is not enough.

So around 3300 BC the scribes of Uruk begin pressing little signs into tablets of wet clay. A sheep. A jar. An ear of barley. And numbers. Along with Egypt's hieroglyphs, this is the oldest writing on Earth. Nobody invented it to write poems. It was invented to keep accounts.

Over the centuries the pictures turn into wedges, pressed in with a cut reed. We call the script cuneiform, from the Latin for wedge. It will be used for more than three thousand years.

[playful] One of the earliest tablets reads, roughly: twenty-nine thousand and eighty-six measures of barley, thirty-seven months, Kushim. The same Kushim appears on eighteen tablets. If it is a name and not a job title, then one of the first people in history whose name we know was an accountant.

### 3. Gods and Neighbours

[warm, confident narrator] By 2900 BC Uruk is not alone. The south is full of cities: Ur, Lagash, Umma, Kish, Nippur. They share one language, Sumerian, and one family of gods, but not one country.

Each city belongs to its god. The god owns the land and the fields, and the ruler is the god's steward. In the middle of each city the temple platform keeps climbing, until it becomes a stepped mountain of brick: the ziggurat.

[serious] Neighbouring gods, it turns out, have neighbour problems. Between Lagash and Umma lies a strip of good farmland called the Gu'edena, the Edge of the Plain. For well over a century the two cities fight over it. It is the earliest war between states that we can follow in writing.

Around 2450 BC Eannatum, ruler of Lagash, wins a great battle and sets up a stone monument to it. His soldiers march in a tight block, shields locked, spears forward. Above them fly the birds that give it its modern name: the Stele of the Vultures. It is the oldest known war monument.

Eannatum marks the border with stones and a ditch. Umma breaks the deal again. And again.

[curious] Somewhere in this world lived a king of Uruk named Gilgamesh. No inscription of his has ever been found. But the Sumerians remembered him as the man who built Uruk's great wall, and later poets made him the hero of the oldest great story ever written down. We will come back to him.

### 4. The First Emperor

[warm, confident narrator] Around 2334 BC a man takes power in the north who will change the rules. He calls himself Sargon. The name means true king, which usually means he was not born one.

[playful] His legend tells it like this. His mother could not keep him. She put her baby in a basket of reeds, sealed it with tar, and set it on the river. A water-drawer named Akki fished him out and raised him as a gardener. If that sounds familiar, you are not the first to notice.

The gardener's boy becomes cupbearer to the king of Kish. Then he overthrows him. Then he defeats Lugalzagesi of Uruk and leads him in a neck-stock to the temple of Enlil at Nippur.

For the first time, the whole land answers to one man. Sargon builds a new capital, Akkad. Archaeologists have never found it.

He makes his daughter high priestess of the moon god at Ur. Her name is Enheduanna. She writes hymns to the goddess Inanna and puts her own name in them. She is usually counted as the first author in history known by name.

His grandson Naram-Sin goes further. He calls himself king of the four quarters of the world, and then a god. He is the first Mesopotamian king to declare himself divine while still alive.

[serious] Then it all falls apart. Around 2200 BC a long drought hits the region. Raiders from the mountains, the Gutians, pour in. A later poem, the Curse of Akkad, blames Naram-Sin for offending Enlil. The great fields gave no grain, it says, and the flooded fields gave no fish.

### 5. Ur

[warm, confident narrator] Out of the wreckage, the old Sumerian city of Ur takes over. Around 2112 BC its king, Ur-Nammu, founds a new dynasty and raises the great ziggurat of Ur. Its lower stages still stand today, in the desert of southern Iraq.

He also leaves the oldest law code that survives, three centuries older than the famous one of Hammurabi. It is surprisingly mild. Most injuries are paid for in silver. Knock out a man's tooth, and you pay two shekels.

His son Shulgi rules for forty-eight years. He builds roads, fixes standard weights, a standard calendar and a standing army. And he records everything. Tens of thousands of tablets survive from this one dynasty: receipts for sheep, lists of workers.

[playful] Shulgi also had himself declared a god, and he was very proud of his legs. One hymn says he ran from Nippur to Ur, about a hundred and sixty kilometres, and back again, in a single day, through a storm, to celebrate two festivals.

[serious] A few decades later it all comes apart. Nomads from the west, the Amorites, push in. Under the last king, Ibbi-Sin, the price of grain rises sixty times. In 2004 BC an army from Elam, in the mountains of Iran, sacks Ur and leads its king away as a prisoner.

Sumerian slowly dies as a spoken language. But it survives in the schools for another two thousand years, the way Latin survived in Europe.

### 6. Hammurabi

[warm, confident narrator] The Amorites settle down and take over the cities. One of their families rules a minor town on the Euphrates. Its name is Babylon.

In 1792 BC a young king named Hammurabi takes its throne. For thirty years he makes allies, then turns on them one by one. By the end of his reign he rules all of southern Mesopotamia, and Babylon will be its capital for well over a thousand years.

He sets up a pillar of black stone more than two metres tall. At the top, Hammurabi stands before Shamash, the sun god of justice. Below are 282 laws.

Some are famous. If a man puts out the eye of another man, his own eye shall be put out. If a house collapses and kills its owner, the builder shall be put to death.

[curious] But read closer. Eye for an eye applies only between equals. If a nobleman blinds a commoner, he pays one mina of silver. If he blinds a slave, he pays half the slave's price. Justice in Babylon depended on who you were.

[playful] And thanks to letters on clay, we can hear ordinary people too. Around 1750 BC in Ur, a man named Nanni sent a tablet to a copper merchant called Ea-nasir. You promised me fine copper, he writes. You took my money, gave me bad ingots, and treated my messenger with contempt. From now on I will accept only fine quality. Archaeologists found more complaints in the house that may have been Ea-nasir's. It is the oldest customer complaint on record.

### 7. Sixty

[warm, confident narrator] To write a letter like that, someone had to go to school. The Sumerians called it the tablet house. Pupils spent years copying signs, word lists and model contracts, and were caned for mistakes.

They also learned mathematics, in a system based on sixty. Sixty divides cleanly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty and thirty. So fractions are easy.

That is why, four thousand years later, an hour still has sixty minutes, a minute has sixty seconds, and a circle has three hundred and sixty degrees.

[curious] Some of their work is startling. A small round school tablet, now at Yale, shows a square with its diagonals and gives the square root of two, accurate to better than one part in a million. Another tablet, called Plimpton 322, lists the sides of right-angled triangles in whole numbers, the rule we call the theorem of Pythagoras. Both are from around 1800 BC. Pythagoras was born more than a thousand years later.

Then there was the sky. Babylonian astronomers watched the moon and the planets night after night and wrote it all down, for centuries. Their reason was omens: the sky carried messages from the gods about the king. But those records became the longest series of observations in the ancient world, and Greek astronomers used them.

### 8. Strangers on the Throne

[serious] After Hammurabi, his kingdom slowly shrinks. Then, in 1595 BC, a Hittite king named Mursili marches all the way from Anatolia, sacks Babylon, and carries off the statue of its god Marduk. Then he simply goes home.

Into the gap come the Kassites, a people from the Zagros mountains. Foreigners with a language of their own, they hold the throne of Babylon for more than four hundred years, the longest dynasty in its history. And they become more Babylonian than the Babylonians: they worship Marduk, write in Akkadian and copy the old texts.

This is a world of great kings. Egypt, the Hittites, Mitanni, Assyria and Babylon write to each other as brothers, in Akkadian cuneiform, the diplomatic language of the age.

[playful] And brothers argue about money. Around 1350 BC the Kassite king Burna-Buriash writes to the pharaoh Akhenaten: I put the forty minas of gold you sent into the furnace, and almost nothing of value came out.

[serious] Around 1200 BC that whole world shakes. The Hittite empire vanishes, and cities fall from Greece to the Levant.

Around 1158 BC Elam raids Babylonia, and the Kassite dynasty soon ends. The Elamite king, Shutruk-Nahhunte, carries trophies home to Susa: the victory stele of Naram-Sin and the law stele of Hammurabi. That is why both were dug up not in Iraq, but in Iran, three thousand years later. Today both stand in the Louvre.

### 9. Assyria

[warm, confident narrator] Up the Tigris, in the north, lies the old trading city of Ashur, named after its god. Its people are the Assyrians.

From the 900s BC their kings march out to war almost every year. They build the most effective army the world has yet seen: chariots, cavalry, siege engines and iron weapons.

Ashurnasirpal the Second builds a new capital at Nimrud. Its palace doors are guarded by giant winged bulls with human heads. When the palace is finished, he throws a party, and lists the guests in stone: 69,574 people, fed for ten days. Among the supplies are ten thousand jars of beer and ten thousand skins of wine.

[serious] The other side of the empire is fear. Assyrian kings boast in stone about the cities they burned and the rebels they punished. And from Tiglath-Pileser the Third onward, they move whole peoples. Rebels are marched to the far end of the empire, and strangers are settled in their place. One estimate puts the total at more than four million people over two and a half centuries. In 720 BC the kingdom of Israel is deported this way, and its ten tribes vanish from history.

In 701 BC Sennacherib invades Judah. His palace reliefs show the siege of the city of Lachish across a whole room. Then he surrounds Jerusalem. His annals boast that he shut up its king, Hezekiah, like a bird in a cage. He never took the city. He took tribute and went home.

### 10. The Library

[warm, confident narrator] The last great king of Assyria is Ashurbanipal, from 668 BC. On his palace walls at Nineveh he hunts lions from his chariot.

But unusually for an Assyrian king, he could read, and he boasted about it. And he collected. His agents copied or seized every text they could find in Babylonia: omens, spells, medicine, dictionaries, epics. At Nineveh he built the greatest library of the ancient Near East.

[serious] Ashurbanipal dies around 631 BC. Within twenty years his empire is gone. In 612 BC an army of Babylonians and Medes storms Nineveh and burns it.

[curious] And the fire is what saved the library. Clay bakes hard in flames, and the falling palace buried the tablets. More than thirty thousand tablets and fragments were dug up in the 1850s and shipped to the British Museum.

In 1872 a young assistant there, George Smith, a former banknote engraver who had taught himself cuneiform, was piecing fragments together. He read about a man warned by a god to build a boat, a great flood, the boat landing on a mountain, and birds sent out to look for dry land. It was a flood story, older than the one in the Bible. According to a later account, he jumped up, rushed about the room, and began to take off his clothes.

It was the eleventh tablet of the Epic of Gilgamesh. In it, the king of Uruk loses his friend Enkidu, sets out to find a way never to die, and fails. He comes home and looks at the wall of his city. In the end, that is what lasts.

### 11. Babylon

[warm, confident narrator] The winner at Nineveh is Babylon. Under Nebuchadnezzar the Second, from 605 BC, it becomes the greatest city of its age.

You enter by the Ishtar Gate, faced with blue glazed bricks and rows of bulls and dragons. A processional way lined with lions leads to the temple of Marduk and its ziggurat, Etemenanki, the house of the foundation of heaven and earth. Its base was about ninety-one metres on each side, and a tablet gives the same figure for its height. To foreigners and exiles it became the Tower of Babel.

[curious] And the Hanging Gardens? No Babylonian text mentions them, and archaeologists have never found them. Some scholars think the Greeks confused them with Sennacherib's gardens at Nineveh. Babylon's most famous wonder may never have stood in Babylon.

[serious] In 587 BC Nebuchadnezzar destroys Jerusalem and its temple and takes its people to Babylon. By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down and wept, says the psalm.

[playful] The last king, Nabonidus, is an odd one. He spends ten years at an oasis in Arabia and leaves his son Belshazzar in charge. He digs under old temples for the foundation stones of ancient kings. When he finds one laid by Naram-Sin, he even tries to work out its date. He is often called the first archaeologist.

In 539 BC the Persian king Cyrus defeats the Babylonian army, and Babylon opens its gates to him. That story has its own episode on this channel. Mesopotamia will never again be ruled by kings of its own.

### 12. What Remains

[warm, confident narrator] Babylon does not die at once. Alexander the Great takes it in 331 BC, and in June 323 he dies there, in the palace of Nebuchadnezzar. We know the day because Babylonian astronomers noted it in their diary of the sky.

His successors build a new Greek city, Seleucia, on the Tigris, and Babylon slowly empties. Ink and the alphabet replace clay and wedges. Only a few temple scholars keep cuneiform alive, for their astronomy. The last tablet we can date is an almanac from Uruk, written around AD 79, the year Vesuvius buried Pompeii. Uruk, where writing began more than three thousand years earlier, is also where it ended.

[serious] Even now, this past is not safe. In April 2003, as Baghdad fell, looters stripped the Iraq Museum. Among the losses was the Warka Vase, carved in Uruk five thousand years ago. In June it came back in pieces, in the back of a car. It has been restored and is on display again.

Look at what they left. Writing. Written law. Sixty minutes in your hour and three hundred and sixty degrees in a circle. The oldest story of a flood, and the oldest story about the fear of death.

All of it began on a plain of mud between two rivers.
