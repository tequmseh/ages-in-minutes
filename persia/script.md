### 0. Cold open

You probably know the Persians as the villains. A faceless army of slaves, driven forward with whips, against three hundred free men in a narrow pass.

That picture comes from the other side. Almost everything we were taught about Persia was written by Greeks, the people Persia was at war with.

Here is what the Greeks tended to leave out. The men who built the Persian capital were paid, and we still have the receipts. Greeks fought on the Persian side too. The Athenian who sank the Persian fleet later moved to Persia and took a job from the king. And the man who finally conquered the empire ended up dressed as a Persian king, running the place almost exactly as he found it.

Persia was the largest state the world had yet seen: from the Balkans to the Indus, dozens of peoples, dozens of languages, about three thousand miles from end to end. It lasted some two hundred and twenty years. That is less time than the United States has existed.

So how do you run an empire three thousand miles wide on horseback? Let's find out. It takes twenty minutes.

### 1. Nobody from nowhere

Around 550 BC, the Middle East is a neighbourhood of old, rich kingdoms. Babylon, with its towers and its bankers. Lydia, where coins were invented. Egypt, already ancient. And the Medes, horse lords of the Iranian plateau.

The Persians are nobody yet. They are herders and horsemen in a dry southern corner of today's Iran, a land called Parsa, and they answer to the Medes. Herodotus says Persian boys were taught three things only: to ride, to shoot a bow, and to tell the truth.

Then one of their kings, Cyrus, starts winning. First he takes over the Medes, and part of the Median army simply changes sides. Next he marches across the whole of Anatolia and takes Lydia, the kingdom of Croesus, whose name still means rich. Then the Greek cities on the coast of what is now Turkey. Then east, into Central Asia.

In about twenty years, a people from the edge of the map owns most of it. One great prize is left.

### 2. Babylon and the cylinder

Babylon is the biggest city on earth, behind walls so thick, Herodotus claims, that a chariot can turn around on top. In 539 BC Cyrus beats its army in the field, and the city opens its gates to him without a siege.

And here he does something unusual. The empires before him, Assyria and Babylon, ruled by terror: beat a people, deport them, smash their temples. Cyrus does the opposite, and he puts it in writing. On a clay cylinder small enough to hold in two hands, in the Babylonians' own language, he announces that their god Marduk personally chose him. He repairs temples. He sends captured statues of gods back to their cities, and deported peoples back to their homes.

Among those who go home are the Jews, exiles in Babylon for half a century. The cylinder never mentions them, but the Bible does. It calls Cyrus, a foreign king, the Lord's anointed.

Today a replica of that cylinder sits at the United Nations, and it is often called the first charter of human rights. It isn't. Nobody in the sixth century BC had heard of human rights, and the curators who look after the real cylinder at the British Museum say so plainly. It is royal PR.

But it is PR with a new idea inside. Cyrus has worked out that it is cheaper to be welcomed than to be feared. Keep your gods. Keep your priests. Keep your laws. Just send the taxes to me.

### 3. Darius takes control of the story

Cyrus dies, Herodotus says, fighting nomads in Central Asia. His son Cambyses adds Egypt, the last of the old great powers, and then dies on the way home. And the whole thing nearly falls apart.

What happens next we know mainly from one man, and he is not neutral. According to Darius, a priest had stolen the throne by pretending to be Cyrus's other son. Darius, a distant relative who carried the king's spear, kills the impostor with six friends and takes the crown. Many historians suspect a simpler story: the impostor was the real prince, and Darius was the usurper.

Either way, the empire explodes. Babylon, Media, Elam, Egypt: provinces rebel one after another. Darius says he fought nineteen battles in a single year and captured nine kings.

Then he does what any modern politician would do. He takes control of the narrative. On a cliff at Behistun, a hundred metres above the main road, he carves his version in three languages, with himself standing on the impostor's chest. Copies go out across the empire. One has turned up in Egypt, on papyrus, in a fourth language.

His enemies, the text says, followed the Lie. He serves the truth. Think of it as a press release carved in stone.

It had a second life, too. In the 1830s and 40s a British officer, Henry Rawlinson, copied it off the cliff face, and those three parallel texts became the key to reading cuneiform.

### 4. The operating system

Cyrus built the empire. Darius turns it into a machine.

He cuts the map into provinces, each run by a governor called a satrap. We still use that word for a boss who behaves like a small king. Herodotus counts twenty of them. Each owes a fixed amount of silver a year. No more "send what you can": a number, written down. Persians grumbled that Cyrus had been a father, and Darius was a shopkeeper.

To keep the satraps honest, Greek writers say, fortress commanders answered straight to the king, and royal inspectors turned up without warning. The Greeks called them the King's Eyes.

Darius mints a gold coin with a royal archer on it, and it becomes the hard currency of its age. For paperwork across the provinces the empire uses Aramaic, which is not Persian at all. It is simply the language most clerks already know. Even at the capital, the accounts are kept in Elamite, the language of the people who were there first. Persian is for monuments.

And nobody is asked to become Persian. Egypt keeps its pharaoh; he just happens to be Darius, carved in Egyptian dress. Babylon keeps its temples, Judea its law, the Greek cities their own local rulers.

### 5. Beating distance

An empire this size has one enemy more dangerous than any rebel: distance. From the capital at Susa to the western end at Sardis is roughly two and a half thousand kilometres. On foot, that is three months.

So Persia organises the road. Much of it is older than the Persians. They inherit it, the way they inherit everything useful. What they add is a system: more than a hundred stations, with fresh horses and fresh riders waiting. A message is passed on like a baton in a relay, and it crosses the empire in something like a week.

Herodotus, who was not easily impressed by Persians, wrote that nothing mortal travels faster, and that neither snow nor rain nor heat nor darkness stops these couriers. If that sounds familiar, a version of it is carved across the front of the main post office in New York.

And in Egypt, Darius links the Nile to the Red Sea with a canal. His inscription there is wonderfully short: I ordered this canal dug; it was dug; ships went from Egypt to Persia, as I wished. An ancestor of the Suez Canal, more than two thousand years early.

### 6. Persepolis and the receipts

To show what all this is for, Darius builds a new ceremonial capital in the Persian homeland. The Greeks will call it Persepolis.

Walk up its great staircase and look at the walls. Assyrian palaces were decorated with sieges, impaled prisoners and piles of heads. Here there is not one battle scene. Instead, twenty-three delegations, carved in stone, climb the stairs with gifts. Lydians with fine cups. Arabs leading a camel. Africans carrying an elephant tusk. An usher takes each leader by the hand. The message is not "we crushed you". It is "everyone has a place, and here it is".

At Susa, Darius lists who built his palace. Cedar from Lebanon. Gold from Sardis and Bactria. Ivory from Africa and India. Greek stonecutters, Egyptian goldsmiths, Babylonian brickmakers. The palace is a map of the empire.

And we can check him. In 1933 archaeologists found an office archive inside the fortification wall of Persepolis: tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, the remains of some fifteen thousand documents, covering just sixteen years. They are receipts. Barley for a gang of foreign workers. Wine for a traveller on his way from India. Feed for horses. Offerings for the gods, and not only the Persian ones.

These workers were not free in our sense. Many were probably deported there. But they were counted, fed and paid, down to the last jug. The most powerful man on earth ran his capital like an accountant.

The Greeks remembered the whips. The tablets show the spreadsheets.

### 7. The price

None of this makes Persia gentle. It is an empire. It takes your silver and your sons. When Darius catches a rebel king, he tells us himself what he did: cut off the man's nose, ears and tongue, put out one eye, chained him at the palace gate for everyone to see, and then impaled him.

Tolerance is the offer. That is what refusing it looks like.

In 499 BC the Greek cities of the Turkish coast refuse. They rebel, with a little help from Athens, and burn Sardis. It takes Persia six years to crush them. The greatest of those cities, Miletus, is sacked and its people deported.

And then the Persian general does something no one expects. He removes the tyrants who had ruled those Greek cities for Persia, and sets up democracies instead. The Great King's general, installing democracy, because it happens to keep the cities quiet. Persia has no ideology except what works.

But Darius has not forgotten Athens.

### 8. Xerxes goes west

In 490 BC a Persian force lands at Marathon, a day's march from Athens, and loses. For Athenians this becomes a founding legend. For Persia it is a failed expedition on a distant frontier, and Darius has bigger problems. Egypt revolts. Then he dies.

His son Xerxes decides to settle the matter properly. The preparations take four years, and they are an engineering show. Across the Hellespont, more than a kilometre of open water, he lays two bridges of boats. And through the neck of the Athos peninsula, where a storm had wrecked an earlier fleet, he digs a ship canal two kilometres long. Later writers called that a Greek tall tale. In the 1990s geophysicists found the canal, buried exactly where Herodotus put it.

How big was the army? Herodotus says one million seven hundred thousand men, drinking rivers dry. Modern historians guess perhaps a tenth of that, which is still enormous.

At Thermopylae it meets the three hundred Spartans, plus several thousand other Greeks who usually get cut from the film. They hold the pass for three days, until a local man shows the Persians a mountain path.

And Xerxes is not short of Greek help. An exiled king of Sparta rides with him as an adviser. Thessaly, Macedon and soon Thebes are in his camp. One of his naval commanders is a queen from a Greek city, Artemisia. A large part of the Greek world is on the Persian side.

### 9. The same war, seen from Susa

Xerxes takes Athens and burns the temples on the Acropolis. For a moment the war looks won.

Then the Greek fleet draws his ships into the narrow strait of Salamis, where numbers are useless, and wrecks them. Xerxes goes home. The army he leaves behind is beaten the next year at Plataea.

For the Greeks, this is the moment that defines them. They will write about it for centuries, and that is why you have heard of it.

Now look at the same events from Susa. The empire has lost a campaign at its far western edge, and over time a strip of coastline. It has not lost a single province that matters. Persia will go on for another hundred and fifty years. No surviving Persian text even mentions the war.

### 10. If you can't beat them, rent them

And Persia learns. If you can't beat the Greeks, rent them.

Themistocles, the Athenian who planned the victory at Salamis, is later driven out by his own city. He flees to the Persian court, learns Persian, and ends his days as a governor for the Great King.

When Athens and Sparta tear each other apart in the Peloponnesian War, Persian gold pays for the Spartan fleet, and that fleet decides the winner. When ten thousand Greek soldiers need work, they march into Asia as hired spears in a Persian civil war.

And in 387 BC the Greek cities, exhausted by yet another war among themselves, accept a peace treaty. Its terms are sent down from Susa, and it is known by the Persian ruler's title: the King's Peace. Its first clause says the Greek cities of Asia belong to him.

What Xerxes lost with an army, his great-grandson wins back with a purse. And the Greeks sign for it.

### 11. The last Achaemenid

The Greeks also tell a story to explain all this: Persia is rotting. Soft kings, scheming queens, too much luxury. It is a good story, and historians repeated it for two thousand years. The evidence says otherwise. In the 340s BC Persia reconquers Egypt, which had broken away for sixty years. The treasury is full. The roads work.

What Persia does not have is an answer to the new army built in Macedon. In 334 BC Alexander leads it into Asia. Three great battles in four years, and the king of kings, Darius the Third, is running east, where one of his own satraps murders him.

Alexander reaches Persepolis and burns the palace: after a long night of drinking, most ancient writers say. Revenge for the Acropolis, a hundred and fifty years late.

But watch what he does next. He gives Darius a royal funeral and hunts down the killer. He keeps the provinces, the taxes, the roads, and a good number of the Persian governors. He starts wearing Persian royal dress. He asks his Macedonians to bow to him the Persian way, and they are appalled. At Susa he marries a daughter of Darius, and has about eighty of his officers marry Persian noblewomen in the same ceremony.

A leading modern historian of Persia has called Alexander "the last of the Achaemenids". He did not destroy the Persian Empire. He took it over. When he died seven years later, his generals carved it up and kept the machinery. Provinces, royal roads, a state courier service: you will meet them again in almost every empire that followed, Rome included.

### 12. What the tablet says

The Persians left words behind as well. Their walled royal parks were called something like pairidaeza. The Greeks heard paradeisos. We say paradise.

Even the name is a souvenir. Persia is what the Greeks called the whole country, after one province, Parsa. The people who live there use a different and very old name, and in 1935 they asked the rest of the world to use it too: Iran.

But the best answer to our question is one of those clay tablets from Persepolis, small enough to hide in your fist. It records that women on a work team have given birth, and are issued extra rations for a month. Double if the baby is a boy. Nobody said this was a fair world.

Think about what that means. Twenty-five centuries ago, in a state stretching across three continents, a clerk knew that a woman in a work gang had just had a child, and that the king owed her wine.

That is how you rule an empire three thousand miles wide on horseback. Not by making everyone the same. By letting them stay different, and writing everything down.

The Greeks won the story. The Persians built the system. Every empire since has borrowed from both.
