### 0. Cold open

[curious] In the middle of Rome there is a concrete roof that is nineteen hundred years old. Rain falls through a hole in the top. Nobody has ever built a bigger dome out of unreinforced concrete.

The people who poured it ruled from northern Britain to the Euphrates. About one person in five on Earth lived under their law.

They started as a few huts on a hill above a river.

In the next twenty minutes: a city that throws out its kings, a single afternoon that kills fifty thousand Romans, a stream that cannot be crossed without starting a war, an emperor who retires to grow cabbages, and a teenager named after Rome's founder who turns out to be the last.

And by the end, you will see why Rome never quite ended. So how did a village on the Tiber end up ruling the known world? Let's find out. It takes twenty minutes.

### 1. The Village on the Tiber

[warm, confident narrator] Start with the map. Halfway down the western side of Italy, the river Tiber winds out of the hills toward the sea. Twenty-five kilometres inland there is a ford, and above it a cluster of steep hills.

The Romans told it like this. On the twenty-first of April, 753 BC, a man named Romulus founded the city. He and his twin brother Remus were sons of the war god Mars, and as babies they were nursed by a she-wolf. The brothers quarrelled over the new walls, and Romulus killed Remus. The city took his name.

[playful] So the official story of Rome begins with one brother murdering the other. Later poets said it explained Rome's endless civil wars.

Archaeology tells a quieter version. There were Iron Age huts on the Palatine Hill long before 753. But on its slope, archaeologists found the remains of a wall dated to the middle of the 700s BC. The wolf is a legend. The date is not far off.

Tradition counts seven kings, with Romulus the first. The last ones came from the Etruscans, the richer cities to the north. They drained the marshy valley between the hills with a great sewer, the Cloaca Maxima. On the drained ground rose the Forum.

Then in 509 BC the Romans drove out their last king, Tarquin the Proud, and swore they would never have another. For the next five hundred years, the word king was an insult in Rome.

Remember that. Five centuries later, it will cost a man his life.

### 2. The Republic

[curious] So who rules instead?

Every year the citizens elect two consuls. Each one can block the other. A senate of former officials gives advice, and in practice it holds the real power. Assemblies of citizens vote on laws and elect the officials. The Romans call it res publica, the public thing. That is where our word republic comes from. Their state signs itself SPQR: the Senate and the People of Rome.

But the people are not equal. A small group of old families, the patricians, holds the offices. Everyone else is a plebeian. Around 494 BC the plebeians simply walk out of the city and refuse to fight. They win their own officials, the tribunes. A tribune can stop almost any act of government with one Latin word: veto. I forbid.

Around 450 BC the laws are finally written down on twelve tablets and displayed in the Forum, so everyone can read what the rules are.

Then in 390 BC disaster comes from the north. An army of Gauls crushes the Romans and takes the city. Only the citadel on the Capitoline Hill holds out. According to legend, the sacred geese of the goddess Juno honked and woke the defenders just in time.

[playful] Rome, saved by geese.

The Gauls leave only after a ransom in gold. When the Romans complain that the weights are rigged, the Gaulish chief throws his sword onto the scales and says: vae victis. Woe to the defeated.

The Romans build a stone wall about eleven kilometres long around their city. No foreign enemy will take Rome again for eight hundred years.

### 3. Italy

[warm, confident narrator] Over the next century and more, Rome fights its neighbours one after another: the Latins, the Etruscans, and the tough mountain tribes of the Samnites.

What makes Rome different is what it does after it wins. It does not wipe out the defeated cities. It turns them into allies. Some get full Roman citizenship, some get part of it. They pay no tribute. Instead, they send soldiers. So every victory makes the Roman army bigger.

To hold it all together, Rome builds roads. In 312 BC an official named Appius Claudius begins a road south to Capua, nearly two hundred kilometres long. It is the Via Appia, and parts of it are still walkable. In the same year he builds the city's first aqueduct.

On such roads a legion can march some thirty kilometres a day. Over the centuries, the network will grow to some eighty thousand kilometres of paved highway.

In 280 BC the Greek city of Tarentum, in the far south, calls for help. Pyrrhus, king of Epirus, lands in Italy with a professional army and war elephants. He beats the Romans twice, but loses so many of his own men that, according to Plutarch, he says: one more victory like that, and we are finished.

Rome simply raises new legions. By 275 Pyrrhus gives up and sails home. Within ten years Rome controls the whole Italian peninsula.

[playful] Pyrrhus won almost every battle and lost the war. Today his name is the word for exactly that: a Pyrrhic victory.

### 4. Carthage

[serious] Now Rome looks across the sea. Just off the toe of Italy lies Sicily, and beyond it Carthage, the richest city in the western Mediterranean, with the strongest navy.

The first war with Carthage starts in 264 BC, over Sicily. Rome has almost no fleet. So it builds one, and adds a boarding bridge with a spike called the raven. It drops onto an enemy deck, and Roman soldiers run across. A sea battle becomes a land battle. After twenty-three years Rome wins Sicily, its first province outside Italy.

In 218 BC the Carthaginian general Hannibal does the unthinkable. He marches from Spain over the Alps, with elephants, into Italy itself. On the second of August, 216 BC, at Cannae, he surrounds the biggest army Rome has ever raised. Livy counts some fifty thousand Roman dead, Polybius seventy thousand. In a single afternoon.

Rome refuses to negotiate. It will not even ransom its prisoners. It raises new legions, and most of its allies stay loyal. The alliance system holds.

A young general, Scipio, takes the war to Africa and beats Hannibal at Zama in 202 BC.

Half a century later, the old senator Cato is still worried about Carthage.

[playful] Plutarch says Cato ended his speeches, whatever the subject, by saying that Carthage must be destroyed.

In 146 BC Rome does exactly that. Carthage is razed. The same year, the Romans destroy the Greek city of Corinth. In time they will call the Mediterranean mare nostrum. Our sea.

### 5. The Republic Breaks

[serious] Conquest pours silver and slaves into Italy, and it breaks the Republic from the inside.

The rich buy up huge estates and work them with slaves. The small farmers who fill the legions are away at war for years. Many come home to find their farms gone, and drift to Rome with nothing.

In 133 BC a tribune named Tiberius Gracchus proposes giving public land to the poor. A crowd of senators and their supporters beats him to death on the Capitol, along with some three hundred of his followers. It is the first political killing in the city in centuries. Ten years later his brother Gaius tries again, and is killed too.

Then the army changes. The general Marius starts taking volunteers who own nothing at all. These men expect their general, not the state, to give them land when they retire. Soldiers now follow a man, not the Republic.

In 88 BC his rival Sulla does something no Roman general has ever done. He marches his legions on Rome itself, and takes it. Six years later, as dictator, he posts lists of enemies who can be killed for a reward. Then he steps down and dies in his bed.

In 73 BC a gladiator named Spartacus breaks out of a training school near Capua. For two years an army of runaway slaves defeats Roman forces up and down Italy before it is finally crushed.

The real question now is simple: who controls the army?

### 6. Caesar

[curious] Three men decide to share the answer. Pompey, the great general. Crassus, the richest man in Rome. And Gaius Julius Caesar, an ambitious politician deep in debt.

Caesar takes command in Gaul, roughly modern France. In eight years of war, from 58 to 50 BC, he conquers it all, and writes his own account of it.

[playful] Caesar wrote about himself in the third person, as if he were a neutral reporter. It was pure propaganda, and schoolchildren still translate it in Latin class.

In 53 BC Crassus is killed fighting the Parthians in the east. Now there are two.

The senate orders Caesar to give up his army. In January 49 BC he stands on the bank of the Rubicon, a small river marking the edge of his province. To cross it with soldiers is to declare war on Rome. According to Suetonius, he says: the die is cast. And crosses.

He wins the civil war. Pompey flees to Egypt and is murdered there. Caesar becomes dictator. The old Roman calendar has drifted months out of step with the seasons. So Caesar makes the year 46 BC last 445 days, then starts a new calendar: 365 days, with a leap year every fourth year. The month of his birth is renamed in his honour: July.

In February 44 BC he is named dictator for life. To many senators, that sounds like a king.

On the Ides of March, the fifteenth, a group of senators surrounds him at a meeting of the senate. More than sixty men are in the plot. He is stabbed twenty-three times. The killers call themselves liberators.

### 7. Augustus

[warm, confident narrator] Caesar's will names an heir: his great-nephew Octavian, eighteen years old.

Thirteen years of civil war follow. In the end it comes down to Octavian against Mark Antony, Caesar's old deputy, and his ally, Queen Cleopatra of Egypt. In 31 BC their fleet is beaten at Actium, in Greece. A year later both are dead, and Egypt, with all its grain, belongs to Rome.

In 27 BC Octavian makes a show of handing power back to the senate. The grateful senate gives him a new name: Augustus, the revered one. He never calls himself king. He is simply princeps, the first citizen. The consuls, the senate and the elections all carry on. He just keeps the army and the money.

[playful] Augustus' real invention was running a monarchy while insisting it was not one.

It works. His rule begins two centuries of relative peace, the Pax Romana. He boasts that he found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble. The month after July is renamed after him: August.

But there is one limit. In AD 9, in the forests of Germany, a chieftain named Arminius, who had served as an officer in the Roman army, leads the governor Varus and three legions into an ambush. All three are wiped out. Suetonius says Augustus beat his head against a door, crying: Varus, give me back my legions! The numbers of those legions, seventeen, eighteen and nineteen, were never used again.

The Rhine becomes the frontier. Augustus dies in AD 14, after more than forty years in power.

### 8. The City

[curious] By now Rome is the biggest city on Earth. Estimates vary, but close to a million people may live here.

Most of them live in apartment blocks five or six storeys high. Water comes from the hills along aqueducts, running mostly underground and flowing by gravity alone. By AD 226 the city has eleven of them, feeding public fountains and huge public baths. One, the Aqua Virgo, built under Augustus, still feeds the Trevi Fountain.

Under Augustus about two hundred thousand citizens receive free grain every month. A century later the poet Juvenal sneered that the Roman people now wanted only two things: bread and circuses.

And the circuses are enormous. At the Circus Maximus, crowds of more than a hundred thousand watch chariot races. In AD 80 the emperor Titus opens a new amphitheatre with a hundred days of games. We call it the Colosseum. It seats about fifty thousand.

A word on the famous thumbs down. The Latin phrase is pollice verso: with a turned thumb. Nobody knows which way it turned. The image of the crowd pointing their thumbs to the ground comes from a French painting of 1872.

[playful] Same with the vomitorium. It was never a room for being sick after a feast. It was an exit. The building, as one Roman writer put it, spewed out its crowd.

Under all of this lies Roman concrete. Around AD 125 the emperor Hadrian rebuilds the Pantheon. Its dome is forty-three metres across. It is the roof from the start of this film.

### 9. The Height

[warm, confident narrator] In AD 117, under the emperor Trajan, the empire reaches its largest size. It runs from northern Britain to the Persian Gulf: about five million square kilometres, and somewhere between sixty and seventy million people.

Trajan's successor Hadrian decides that is enough. He pulls back in the east and fixes the borders. In Britain, from AD 122, soldiers build a stone wall a hundred and seventeen kilometres long, coast to coast. Some thirty legions now guard the frontiers.

Being Roman no longer means being from Rome. Trajan and Hadrian come from Roman families settled in Spain. A later emperor, Septimius Severus, is born in Libya. And in AD 212 the emperor Caracalla grants citizenship to almost every free person in the empire. A shepherd in Syria and a merchant in Gaul can now say the same words: I am a Roman citizen.

It is not all golden. In the 160s a plague sweeps the empire. And the emperor Marcus Aurelius spends years in army camps on the Danube, fighting Germanic tribes.

In camp, he writes private notes to himself, in Greek. They are about duty, death and staying calm.

[playful] One of them says: every morning, remind yourself that today you will meet people who are rude and ungrateful. Eighteen centuries later, that book is sold in airport bookshops.

When Marcus Aurelius dies in 180, the long peace starts to end.

### 10. The Crisis

[serious] In 235 the emperor Severus Alexander is murdered by his own soldiers. What follows is fifty years of chaos.

Between 235 and 284 more than twenty emperors are recognised in Rome, and many more claim the title. Most last a year or two. Most are killed by their own troops.

Goths cross the Danube. In the east a new Persian dynasty, the Sasanians, attacks Roman Syria. In 260, near Edessa, the Persian king Shapur takes the emperor Valerian prisoner. It is the first time a Roman emperor is captured by a foreign enemy. Shapur has the scene carved into a cliff near Persepolis. It is still there.

The empire splits in three. A breakaway Gallic Empire in the west. In the east, the desert city of Palmyra, led by its queen Zenobia. And Rome in the middle.

The emperor Aurelian puts it back together in the early 270s. He also gives Rome new walls, nineteen kilometres of brick.

Then in 284 a soldier from Dalmatia, Diocletian, takes power. He decides one man cannot rule all of this. He splits the empire between four rulers, two senior and two junior. He doubles the number of provinces and even tries to fix the price of over a thousand goods by law.

In 305 he does what no emperor had done before. He retires. When he is asked to come back and take power again, he answers, according to a later account: if you could see the cabbages I have grown with my own hands, you would never ask me.

### 11. The Cross

[curious] Meanwhile a small religion from Judaea has been spreading through the cities of the empire: Christianity. At times its followers are persecuted. Diocletian launches the worst persecution of all.

Then in 312 the emperor Constantine fights a rival for Rome at the Milvian Bridge, just outside Rome. Christian writers say that before the battle he saw a sign from God, and ordered the symbol of Christ painted on his soldiers' shields. He wins.

In 313 Constantine and his co-emperor grant freedom of worship to all, Christians included. In 325 Constantine presides over the first great council of bishops at Nicaea, which lays the basis of a creed still recited in churches today.

And he builds a new capital. On the Bosporus, on the site of the Greek town of Byzantium, between Europe and Asia, close to the dangerous frontiers on the Danube and in the east. In 330 he dedicates it: Constantinople. A new Rome.

[playful] Constantine was baptised only on his deathbed, in 337. Whatever else he was, he was careful.

In 380 the emperor Theodosius makes Christianity the official religion of the empire. The old temples are closed, and sacrifices to the gods are banned.

When Theodosius dies in 395, the empire is divided between his two sons. One rules the east from Constantinople, the other the west. After that, no single emperor will ever rule the whole of it again.

### 12. What Remains

[serious] In 376 a people called the Goths, fleeing the Huns, ask to cross the Danube into the empire. They are let in, then mistreated. They rebel. In 378, at Adrianople, they destroy a Roman army and kill the emperor Valens.

On the twenty-fourth of August, 410, the Visigoths under their king Alaric enter Rome and sack it. The first foreign enemy inside the city in eight hundred years, since the Gauls. In Bethlehem, the scholar Jerome writes: the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken.

In 455 the Vandals sack Rome again. They sail in from their new kingdom in North Africa. Its capital is Carthage.

In 476 a Germanic officer named Odoacer removes the last emperor of the West, a teenager named Romulus Augustus. Romans call him Augustulus, little Augustus. Odoacer does not even bother to take the title. He sends the imperial regalia to Constantinople.

[playful] The last emperor of the West was named after the founder of Rome and its first emperor. History has a sense of humour.

But only the West has fallen. In Constantinople the empire goes on for almost another thousand years, until 1453, and its people still call themselves Romans.

And the rest of Rome never left. More than nine hundred million people speak languages that grew out of Latin. The letters on this screen are its alphabet. Your calendar is Caesar's, down to July and August. Senate, republic, veto: all Latin. And on the streets of Rome, the manhole covers still say SPQR.

A village of huts on the Tiber became an empire. The empire fell. The idea of Rome never did.
