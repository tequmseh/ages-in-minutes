### 0. Cold open

[curious] Summer, 480 BC. A Persian horseman rides up to a narrow pass in central Greece. Behind him marches one of the largest armies the ancient world ever assembled. In front of him stand a stone wall and a few thousand Greeks.

He watches the men posted outside the wall. Some are exercising naked. Others are calmly combing their long hair.

The scout rides back to King Xerxes, confused. A Spartan exile in the royal camp explains: these men always arrange their hair when they are about to risk their lives.

These are the Spartans, the most feared soldiers in Greece. And yet, about a hundred and fifty years after this day, their city will have fewer than a thousand citizens left.

In the next twenty minutes: a state that inspected newborn babies, boys who were trained to steal, women who owned land and won at Olympia, an enslaved people who far outnumbered their masters, and the real story of the three hundred.

[serious] So who were the Spartans, and how did the most feared army in Greece fade away? Let's find out. It takes twenty minutes.

### 1. A Valley Without Walls

[warm, confident narrator] Sparta lies in the far south of Greece, in the Peloponnese. The region is called Laconia: a long green valley along the river Eurotas, shut in by mountains, Taygetus to the west and Parnon to the east.

The Greeks knew it from Homer. In the Iliad this is the kingdom of Menelaus, husband of Helen of Troy. And there really was a Bronze Age palace here. Since 2009, archaeologists have been digging one at Agios Vasileios, about twelve kilometres from Sparta, complete with clay tablets in the earliest Greek script, Linear B. In the fourteenth century BC it burned down.

Then the palaces of Greece collapse, and for about two hundred years the valley goes almost quiet. Around 1000 BC new settlers appear. They speak a Greek dialect called Doric, and later they will claim the hero Heracles as their ancestor.

They do not build a city. They build four villages side by side: Pitana, Mesoa, Limnai and Kynosoura. Soon a fifth joins them, Amyklai, five kilometres to the south. Together these villages are Sparta.

[curious] From the start, Sparta has an oddity. It has two kings at the same time, from two royal families, the Agiads and the Eurypontids. Both claim descent from Heracles, and each can hold the other back.

[serious] And for most of its history, Sparta has no city wall at all. Its men are its walls.

### 2. The Land Behind the Mountain

[warm, confident narrator] The Eurotas valley is good land, but there is not enough of it. On the other side of Mount Taygetus lies a wider, richer plain: Messenia.

In the late eighth century BC, Sparta goes to war for it. Tradition dates the war from 743 to 724 BC. A Spartan poet, Tyrtaeus, says it lasted nineteen years.

The Messenians are not wiped out or driven away. Most of them stay on their land, and that is worse. They become helots. A helot is not a slave you buy at a market. He belongs to the Spartan state and is tied to his plot. In the words of Tyrtaeus, the helots are like donkeys worn down by heavy loads, carrying to their masters half of everything their fields produce.

About two generations later, in the seventh century, the Messenians rise up. The second war is long and desperate, but Sparta wins again, and the helots stay helots.

[serious] Between the Spartans and the helots there is a third group: the perioikoi, "those who live around". They are free Greeks in the towns of Laconia and Messenia. They trade, they make the Spartans' weapons and armour, and they fight in the army. But they have no say in how Sparta is run.

[curious] So by about 650 BC, Sparta owns two fertile regions and the people who farm them. That is a fortune. It is also a trap. The masters are few, and the helots are many. Almost everything Sparta becomes next is built around that one problem.

### 3. The Lawgiver Who May Never Have Lived

[warm, confident narrator] The Spartans said their way of life came from one man, Lycurgus. He asked the oracle of Apollo at Delphi for guidance, they said, and came home with a set of laws. Even ancient writers could not agree on when he lived, and many historians today think he is a legend. But the system named after him was real.

At the top stand the two kings, who lead the army. Beside them sits a council of twenty-eight elders. Every one of them is over sixty, and they serve for life. Below them, the assembly of citizens decides by shouting, and the loudest roar wins.

And watching everyone, even the kings, are five ephors, overseers elected for a single year.

Each citizen, Plutarch says, received a plot of land worked by helots. Lycurgus banned gold and silver coins. Money was made of iron instead, so heavy that a modest sum needed a storeroom to keep and a pair of oxen to move.

Every citizen ate in a common mess, and each had to pay his share of the food from his own land. A man who could not pay lost his citizenship.

The famous dish was black broth: pork boiled in its own blood, with vinegar. A visitor from Sybaris, a city famous for luxury, tasted it and said that now he understood why Spartans were not afraid to die.

[serious] A Spartan citizen did not farm, trade or make anything. He had one job: to be a soldier. And the making of a soldier began at birth.

### 4. Raised by the State

[warm, confident narrator] According to Plutarch, a Spartan father did not decide whether to raise his son. He carried the newborn to the elders, who examined it. A sturdy child was raised. A weak one, Plutarch says, was taken to a place at the foot of Mount Taygetus.

[curious] But when researchers from the University of Athens studied the remains from a pit at the foot of Taygetus, the one usually linked to the legend, they found forty-six people, all aged roughly eighteen to thirty-five. There were no babies. The lead researcher called the story probably a myth, because its ancient sources are rare, late and vague.

[warm, confident narrator] At seven, a boy left home. He joined a pack of boys his own age, led by an older youth. His hair was cut short and he went barefoot. From the age of twelve he got one cloak for the whole year, and he slept on reeds that he pulled from the riverbank with his bare hands.

He was kept hungry on purpose and encouraged to steal food. If he was caught, he was beaten. Not for stealing, but for getting caught.

At the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia, cheeses were piled on the altar. Boys tried to snatch them while men stood guard with whips.

[serious] At twenty a young Spartan entered the army. Until thirty he still belonged to his unit more than to his family. Only after all this, and only if he could pay his share at the mess, was he a full citizen.

### 5. The Women of Sparta

[warm, confident narrator] In most Greek cities, a respectable woman spent her life indoors. In Sparta, girls ran races, wrestled, and threw the discus and the javelin. Xenophon writes that Lycurgus wanted women to exercise as much as men, and set up contests for them. The logic was blunt: strong mothers make strong soldiers.

Women ran the households. They could own and inherit land. Aristotle complains that nearly two fifths of all Spartan land belongs to women.

Plutarch tells a story about Gorgo, the daughter of one king and the wife of another, Leonidas. A woman from Athens asked her why Spartan women were the only ones who ruled their men. Because, Gorgo answered, we are the only ones who give birth to men.

[curious] In 396 BC a Spartan princess named Cynisca, sister of King Agesilaus, entered a four-horse chariot at the Olympic Games. Women could not drive in the race, but the victory went to the owner of the horses, not to the driver. Her team won. In 392 BC it won again.

Cynisca became the first woman ever to win at Olympia. Her inscription boasts that she was the only woman in all of Greece to win that crown.

[serious] And the famous farewell, the Spartan mother telling her son to come back with his shield, or on it? It comes from a collection of sayings put together by Plutarch, more than five hundred years after Sparta's great age. It may simply be what other Greeks liked to believe about Spartan women.

### 6. The Enemy at Home

[serious] Now the dark side. At the battle of Plataea, in 479 BC, Herodotus counts seven helots for every Spartan citizen in the army. The Athenian historian Thucydides writes that most Spartan institutions were designed with one aim: to guard against the helots.

Aristotle reported that every year, as soon as the new ephors took office, they formally declared war on the helots. That way, killing a helot was not murder in the eyes of the gods.

Then there was the krypteia, the secret service. Young men were sent out into the countryside. By day, Plutarch writes, they hid. By night, they came down to the roads and killed any helot they caught. Historians still argue over what exactly it was.

[curious] Thucydides tells of an even darker moment, though he does not say exactly when it happened. Sparta announced that helots who had fought bravely for the state could claim their freedom. About two thousand stepped forward. They put on garlands and went around the temples as free men. Soon afterwards, every one of them vanished. Nobody ever learned how each of them was killed.

[serious] The fear ran both ways. In 464 BC a great earthquake struck Sparta. The helots of Messenia rose up and dug in on Mount Ithome, and the revolt dragged on for years.

Athens, still Sparta's ally, sent hoplites to help. Then Sparta, alone among all its allies, sent the Athenians home, afraid they might side with the rebels. The two cities were never friends again.

### 7. Masters of Greece

[warm, confident narrator] By the sixth century BC, Sparta stands at the head of an alliance of cities across the Peloponnese. Historians call it the Peloponnesian League.

Meanwhile, across the Aegean, Persia has reached the Greek world.

[curious] Around 499 BC, Aristagoras of Miletus arrives in Sparta. The Greeks of Ionia are rising against Persia, and they need Spartan soldiers. He brings King Cleomenes a bronze tablet engraved with a map of the whole world.

Cleomenes asks one question: how far is it from the sea to the king's capital? A three months' march, Aristagoras admits. The meeting is over.

Aristagoras follows the king to his house and starts offering money: ten talents, then more, then fifty. Cleomenes' daughter Gorgo, eight or nine years old, is standing in the room. Father, she says, this stranger will corrupt you. Go away. Cleomenes leaves the room, and Aristagoras leaves Sparta without an army.

[serious] A few years later, the Persian king Darius sends heralds to demand earth and water, the symbols of surrender. In Athens they are thrown into a pit. In Sparta they are thrown down a well and told to take their earth and water from there.

In 490 BC the Persians land at Marathon, and Athens begs Sparta for help. The Spartans agree, but say they cannot march before the full moon. Then two thousand of them cover the road to Attica in three days, and still arrive too late. They look at the Persian dead, praise the Athenians, and go home.

### 8. The Hot Gates

[warm, confident narrator] Ten years later, in 480 BC, Darius's son Xerxes invades Greece with an enormous army and fleet. The Greeks decide to stop him at Thermopylae, the Hot Gates, a narrow pass between the mountains and the sea.

Busy with a festival, Sparta sends only an advance force under King Leonidas: three hundred Spartan citizens, every one of them a father with a living son. Together with the allies, the army in the pass numbers around seven thousand men.

[serious] For two days the Greeks hold the pass.

Then a local Greek named Ephialtes tells the Persians about a mountain path that leads around the pass. That night, Xerxes's elite troops, the Immortals, take it.

At dawn on the third day, Leonidas learns that he is being surrounded. He sends most of the army away. He stays with his three hundred, with seven hundred men from the small city of Thespiae who refuse to leave, and with four hundred Thebans. The Thebans later surrender. The rest fight until they are all killed.

[curious] Herodotus records one line. A local man warns that the Persian archers are so many, their arrows hide the sun. The Spartan Dieneces answers: good news, then we will fight in the shade.

The more famous line, Leonidas telling Xerxes to come and take their weapons, appears only in Plutarch, almost six hundred years later.

Over the fallen stands a short epitaph: Stranger, tell the Spartans that we lie here, obedient to their words.

[serious] Thermopylae was a defeat. But it made the Spartans a legend.

### 9. Plataea and Pride

[warm, confident narrator] Soon after, the Greek fleet smashes the Persian navy at Salamis. Xerxes goes home, but he leaves his general Mardonius in Greece with a large army.

In 479 BC the Greeks meet it at Plataea. This time Sparta sends almost everything: five thousand citizens, five thousand perioikoi and thirty-five thousand helots. The commander of the whole Greek army is Pausanias, nephew of Leonidas and regent for Leonidas's young son.

When the battle finally comes, it is the Spartans who meet the best Persian troops. A Spartan named Arimnestus kills Mardonius, his guard falls around him, and the Persian army breaks. Persia never invades mainland Greece again.

[serious] Sparta now has the most famous army in the world, and a general who cannot handle the fame. Pausanias starts dressing like a Persian noble and acting like a king. He has his own name inscribed on the victory offering at Delphi. Then he is caught writing secret letters to Xerxes.

When the ephors come to arrest him, he runs into a small building at the temple of Athena of the Bronze House. They take off the roof, wall up the door and wait. When he is about to die of hunger, they carry him out, so that his death will not defile the temple.

[curious] After this, Sparta stops sending its commanders overseas, afraid that they will be corrupted like Pausanias. The war against Persia at sea passes to Athens, and Athens turns its alliance into an empire.

### 10. The War with Athens

[warm, confident narrator] For fifty years Greece has two masters: Athens at sea and Sparta on land. In 431 BC they go to war. With one pause, the war will last twenty-seven years. We call it the Peloponnesian War.

Sparta's plan is simple. Every summer its army burns the farms around Athens. The Athenians sit behind their Long Walls, which reach all the way to the harbour, and live on grain brought in by sea.

[curious] Then, in 425 BC, the unthinkable happens. On the little island of Sphacteria, off the coast of Messenia, the Athenians trap a Spartan force. Two hundred and ninety-two men lay down their shields, and about a hundred and twenty of them are full Spartan citizens. Greece is stunned. Spartans were supposed to die, not surrender.

[serious] In the end, Sparta wins with Persian money. A Persian prince, Cyrus the Younger, pays for a Spartan fleet. Its admiral, Lysander, catches the Athenian fleet on the beach at Aegospotami in 405 BC and captures almost all of it.

Starving, Athens surrenders in 404. Sparta refuses to destroy it, saying it will not enslave a Greek city that did so much for Greece in its hour of danger. Instead, the walls of Athens are pulled down to the music of flute girls.

[curious] Seventeen years later, in 387 BC, Sparta accepts a peace dictated by the Persian king. The Greek cities of Asia, the very cities Sparta had refused to help in 499, go back to Persia. Sparta stays on top, as the king's guardian of the peace in Greece.

### 11. One Afternoon at Leuctra

[serious] Behind the power, Sparta is shrinking. In 480 BC, an exiled Spartan king told Xerxes that Sparta had about eight thousand fighting men.

The system itself is to blame. Citizenship depends on land and on paying your share at the mess. Land drifts into fewer and fewer hands, much of it to heiresses. A man who becomes too poor to pay stops being a citizen.

[warm, confident narrator] In 371 BC a Spartan army under King Cleombrotus meets the Thebans at Leuctra, in central Greece. The Theban general Epaminondas tries something new. Against the usual Spartan line, twelve men deep, he stacks his left wing fifty shields deep and aims it straight at the king.

The Spartan right collapses. Cleombrotus is killed. Of the seven hundred Spartan citizens on the field, four hundred die. In one afternoon Sparta loses more than half the citizens it brought, and the myth that it cannot be beaten.

[curious] Xenophon writes that the next day, the families of the dead walked about Sparta with cheerful faces, while the few relatives of survivors who showed themselves looked gloomy and downcast.

[serious] Then the Thebans march south into Laconia itself, and on into Messenia, which they set free. In 369 BC a new city, Messene, rises at the foot of Mount Ithome. After more than three centuries, the helots of Messenia are free, and Sparta has lost the western and better half of its land.

A generation later, Aristotle writes that Sparta's territory could feed thirty thousand soldiers, and yet the number of Spartan citizens has fallen below one thousand.

### 12. What Remains

[warm, confident narrator] Sparta never gets its power back, but it keeps its pride. When Philip of Macedon conquers Greece, he warns the Spartans: if I invade Laconia, I will drive you out. The ephors reply with a single word: If. Philip does ravage the country, but he never takes the city.

In 334 BC his son Alexander sends three hundred suits of Persian armour to Athens, with an inscription: from Alexander, son of Philip, and the Greeks, except the Spartans.

[serious] More than a century later, Nabis, the last strong ruler of an independent Sparta, frees many helots. After his death, in 188 BC, the Achaean League tears down the city's new walls and abolishes the old training.

[curious] Then comes Rome. After 146 BC, Sparta is a free city in the Roman world, and it finds a new career: tourism. At the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia, young men are now flogged at the altar, sometimes to death, while visitors watch. In the third century AD the Romans build a theatre around the altar, so that more people can see.

In AD 396 the Goths of Alaric sack the city. Over the centuries Sparta shrinks to a village among ruins. In 1834 Otto, the first king of modern Greece, orders a new town built on the old site.

[warm, confident narrator] Thucydides once imagined Sparta deserted, with nothing left but its temples and foundations. People, he wrote, would struggle to believe how powerful it had been. He was right. Sparta left very few monuments.

What it left was words. Laconic, from Laconia, still means saying a lot in very few. And the idea of Sparta, the city of discipline and the three hundred in the pass, has outlived the city itself.
